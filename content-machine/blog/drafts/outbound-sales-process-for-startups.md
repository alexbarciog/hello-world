An outbound sales process for startups has eight steps: write a one-page customer profile from your last ten conversations, build a list of 100 accounts, watch those accounts for a buying signal, send one short message within a day of the signal, follow up on a fixed schedule, qualify in the first reply, track every account through five pipeline stages in a sheet, and review the numbers every Friday. One founder can run it in about an hour a day on LinkedIn and email. The exact searches, messages, stage table and a worked example follow.

## What you need to run an outbound sales process at a startup

You need less than the tool lists suggest. The process below was built to run with the following and nothing else:

| Item | Why | Cost to start |
|---|---|---|
| A LinkedIn account with a complete profile, used daily for at least four weeks | Cold connection requests from a thin or new profile get ignored or flagged | Free |
| A work email on your own domain, with the usual sending authentication set up by whoever manages your DNS | Replies to a personal address look like spam | Usually included with your domain |
| One spreadsheet (Google Sheets or Excel) | This is the CRM until step 8 says otherwise | Free |
| A calendar booking link | Removes two back-and-forth emails per meeting | Free tiers exist |
| 60 to 90 minutes a day, at the same time every day | Outbound works on consistency, not bursts | Your time |

A paid data tool, a sequencer and a proper CRM come later, if the weekly review in step 8 says the process works. Buying them first is the most common way startups spend money on outbound before they know what to say.

## Step 1: Write the customer profile from your last ten conversations

Open a blank page and list the last ten people who took a call with you, bought, or almost bought. For each one, write the company size, the person's title, what was happening at the company when they talked to you, and the sentence they used to describe the problem. The repeated answers are your ideal customer profile; everything else is noise.

Then turn the profile into three filters you can type into a search box: an industry or a product category, a headcount band, and one or two titles. If you have fewer than ten conversations, use the people who replied to anything you ever sent, and treat the profile as a hypothesis to test in the next four weeks. The lesson on [what an ideal customer profile is](/learn/what-is-an-ideal-customer-profile) has a worksheet if you want the longer version.

Write the profile as one sentence you can say out loud. Example of the format: "Heads of sales at 20 to 200 person B2B services firms who have just hired their first SDR." If the sentence has more than one "or" in it, you have two profiles. Pick one for the first month.

## Step 2: Build a list of 100 accounts, not 10,000

A hundred accounts is the right size for a founder's first list. It is small enough that you can read every reply and remember every company, and large enough that the numbers in step 8 mean something. The ranking guides agree on this range and then recommend buying a database; you do not need one yet.

Build the list in LinkedIn's free search. Use the people search for the titles and the company search for the firmographics, and paste each result into the sheet with the company name, the person's name and title, the profile URL and the date you added them.

| What you want | Where to type it | Search string |
|---|---|---|
| People with the right title at the right kind of company | People search, Title filter plus keywords | `("head of sales" OR "VP sales" OR "sales director") AND NOT recruiter` |
| Companies in the headcount band | Company search, Company size filter | Industry keyword, then tick the 11-50 and 51-200 bands |
| People who recently changed job into the title | People search, keywords box | `"head of sales" "new role"` sorted by recent activity, or check the "Started new position" note on the profile |
| Companies hiring for the role you sell to or around | People search, keywords box | `"we're hiring" "SDR"` with the Posts tab selected |

Two rules for the sheet. First, one row per person, never per company, because the follow-up in step 5 is to a person. Second, add a column called "Why them" and fill it in one short phrase for every row ("hiring two SDRs", "posted about reply rates dropping"). If you cannot fill that column, the row does not belong on the list.

## Step 3: Watch the 100 accounts for a signal before you write

The difference between a startup's outbound that gets replies and one that does not is almost always timing, not copy. A head of sales who posted yesterday that cold email stopped working is reachable this week; the same person in a quiet quarter is not. So the process waits for a signal on each account before the first message goes out, and leads with that signal.

For a 100-account list, three signals are enough: the person or the company posted about the problem you solve, the company is hiring for a role your product affects, or a decision-maker engaged with a competitor's or an industry leader's post. Save a LinkedIn post search for each one, filtered to the past week, and check them at the start of your daily hour. The guide on [how to set up signal-based outbound](/guides/set-up-signal-based-outbound) has the full set of search strings and the signal-log layout, so this page keeps to the process.

Checking saved searches by hand is where the daily hour goes if you let it. Intentsly's [LinkedIn signals](/features/linkedin-signals) do this step for you: it watches post engagement, keyword posts and competitor followers for your profile and scores each person against it, so you open a ranked list instead of running searches. The process is the same either way; the tool only changes who runs the searches.

If an account shows no signal after four weeks, it stays on the list and gets one plain message anyway, so the list keeps moving. Signal-first means signal when there is one, not silence when there is not.

## Step 4: Send the first touch within a day of the signal

The first message has one job: to show that you noticed something specific and to ask a question that is easy to answer. It does not pitch, it does not attach a deck, and it does not ask for thirty minutes. Keep it under 60 words on LinkedIn and under 100 in email.

LinkedIn connection note (the note field is short, so every word has to earn its place):

```
Saw your post on reply rates falling since the summer. We hit the same wall at [your company] and changed how we pick who to message. Happy to compare notes if useful.
```

Email first touch:

```
Subject: your SDR hire

Hi [first name],

Saw [company] is hiring an SDR. When we did the same, the hardest part was not the hire but giving them a list worth working on the first Monday.

Curious how you are planning the first month's list. If it is useful, I can share the one-page process we ended up with.

[Your name]
[Your company], [city]
```

Send the LinkedIn note and the email the same day. On LinkedIn, stay under the platform's patience: at Intentsly we cap each account at 30 connection requests and 30 messages a day and recommend 15, because a restriction on a founder's own profile costs far more than the extra requests gain. If you prefer calling, go in with open eyes: Cognism's own study of its calls puts the average cold-calling success rate at 2.3 percent in 2025, down from 4.82 percent the year before ([Cognism, opened 1 November 2026](https://cognism.com/blog/cold-calling-success-rates)), so for a one-person process the phone is a follow-up channel, not the opener.

For email in the United States, the rules are short and worth following from day one: an accurate sender and subject line, your physical postal address in the message, a working way to opt out, and opt-outs honored within 10 business days; the FTC's guide lists a penalty of up to $53,088 per email that breaks them ([FTC, CAN-SPAM compliance guide](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/can-spam-act-compliance-guide-business)). Put the address and an opt-out line in your signature once and you are done.

## Step 5: Follow up on a fixed schedule

Replies tend to arrive on the second or third touch rather than the first, and the fixed schedule is what makes the process a process rather than a mood. Write the schedule into the sheet as columns, and move each row forward every morning.

| Touch | Day | Channel | What it says |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0 | LinkedIn note plus email | The signal and one question (step 4) |
| 2 | 3 | Email reply to touch 1 | One sentence of new context: a number, a short story, a question rephrased |
| 3 | 7 | LinkedIn message (if connected) or email | A specific offer: the one-page process, a teardown of their current setup, a relevant example |
| 4 | 14 | Email | The close-out: "I'll stop here; if this becomes a priority, reply to this thread" |

Four touches over two weeks, then the row moves to "Closed: no response" and stays there for 90 days. Do not add a fifth touch to feel thorough; add it only if the step 8 numbers show the fourth touch is still producing replies.

Each follow-up must add something. "Just bumping this" adds nothing and trains the reader to ignore you. If you have nothing new to say, send the close-out early.

## Step 6: Qualify in the first reply, not the first call

A reply is not a lead. The founders who burn their first month on outbound are usually the ones who took every "sure, send me more" as a meeting. Before you offer a time, ask one qualifying question in the thread, and only book when the answer shows the person has the problem now and can do something about it.

The question that works best in writing is about the current state: "How are you handling the list for the new SDR today?" The answer tells you whether there is a problem, who owns it and how urgent it is, in one message. If the answer is "we're fine, just curious", thank them, add a note, and move the row to "Nurture". A five-minute check before you send the calendar link is in [how to qualify leads before outreach](/guides/qualify-leads-before-outreach); it is written for the pre-send step but the same four questions work in a reply thread.

Book the meeting with your calendar link in the same message as your reply to a good answer. Every extra message between "yes" and a booked slot loses meetings.

## Step 7: Track every account through five pipeline stages

The sheet is the process. Paste this table into a second tab and give every row on the list one of these stages; the whole weekly review in step 8 reads from this column.

| Stage | Entry criterion | Exit criterion | Time limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Listed | Row added with "Why them" filled in | A signal is logged, or four weeks pass | 4 weeks |
| Contacted | Touch 1 sent, date logged | A reply, or touch 4 sent | 14 days |
| Replied | Any human reply | Qualifying question answered | 7 days |
| Qualified | Problem exists now and the person can act on it | Meeting held | 14 days |
| Meeting held | Call took place | Next step agreed with a date, or closed | 14 days |

Add two terminal states, "Closed: not now" with a revisit date and "Closed: never", and a "Nurture" state for warm people with no current problem. Every row has exactly one stage and a date it entered that stage. If a row passes its time limit, move it, do not let it sit.

The time limits are what keep a one-person pipeline honest. Without them, "Replied" fills up with people who said "maybe next quarter" in March, and the pipeline looks healthy while nothing closes.

## Step 8: Review weekly and decide: fix, scale or hire

Every Friday, count the rows that entered each stage this week and write the five numbers on one line in the sheet: listed, contacted, replied, qualified, meetings held. After four weeks you have the only benchmarks that matter for your startup: your own. Compare week to week, not to figures from vendor blogs.

Read the numbers from the bottom up, and fix one thing at a time:

1. Meetings held is low but Qualified is fine: the booking step is leaking. Put the calendar link in the reply and confirm the day before.
2. Qualified is low but Replied is fine: the list or the profile is off. Reread the "Why them" column; tighten the profile in step 1.
3. Replied is low but Contacted is fine: the timing or the first message is off. Check whether touch 1 actually named the signal; rewrite the opener, not the follow-ups.
4. Contacted is low: you are not doing the daily hour. Block it in the calendar or the rest does not matter.

The hiring rule: do not hire an SDR to find out whether outbound works. Hire one when the sheet shows four consecutive weeks of meetings from the process, the opener and the profile have not changed in those four weeks, and your own time on the daily hour is now the thing capping the numbers. Until all three are true, a hire inherits a process that is still changing under them.

## A worked example: Lumen Ledger's first month

Lumen Ledger is a hypothetical example, a four-person startup selling bookkeeping software to small accounting firms, invented here to show the process with counts. The founder, Priya, ran the eight steps alone, one hour a day, for four weeks.

Week 1: she wrote the profile ("owners of 5 to 30 person accounting firms who have posted about month-end close") and built a list of 100 owners from the people search and the company search in step 2, filling the "Why them" column for every row. Her saved post searches caught 11 signals that week; she sent 11 first touches.

Week 2: 23 more signals, 23 first touches, and the first replies, 6 of them, came in on touches 2 and 3 from week 1. She asked the qualifying question in each thread; 3 answered with a current problem and booked. By Friday the stage column read 66 Listed, 28 Contacted, 3 Replied, 3 Qualified.

Weeks 3 and 4: she kept to the schedule, sent the first plain messages to accounts that had shown no signal in four weeks, and held 7 meetings in total by the end of the month, 2 of which turned into paid pilots. Her Friday line for week 4 showed where the leak was: Qualified was healthy, but Replied was thin on the no-signal rows, so she kept signal-first and dropped the plain messages. None of these numbers are a benchmark; they are what a sheet looks like when the process is followed.

For comparison, the one first-hand run we can cite: when we launched Intentsly we ran our own signals and AI SDR on LinkedIn, targeting founders posting about cold outreach burnout, and booked 14 qualified demos in 14 days with zero manual outreach, at a reply rate 4x our previous cold campaigns. The process was the one above; the difference was that steps 3 to 5, and the replies, ran without us.

## Where this goes wrong

The three mistakes that account for most failed startup outbound, in the order they usually happen:

1. **Buying the list before writing the profile.** A 5,000-row export from a data tool feels like progress and produces a month of messages to people who were never going to buy. Do step 1, then build 100 rows by hand, then decide whether you need a tool.
2. **Leading with the product instead of the signal.** "We help companies like yours" is the sentence that gets the archive click. If touch 1 does not name something the reader did or said this month, it is a cold message with extra steps.
3. **Skipping the weekly line.** Without the five Friday numbers, every decision about the process is a feeling, and founders stop outbound in week three because it "doesn't feel like it's working" when the sheet would have shown the replies arriving on schedule.

A fourth, quieter one: sending more than the account can bear. LinkedIn restrictions on a founder's own profile take weeks to recover from and cost more than the few extra requests gained. Stay under the caps in step 4.

## What to do with the list

Once the sheet has rows in every stage, the list becomes three lists, each with its own action. Rows in Qualified and Meeting held get your calendar and your full attention; nothing else on the list matters more than the next step with each of them. Rows in Nurture and Closed: not now get a revisit date and one useful message when their signal fires again, which is why the saved searches stay on even after the first month. Rows in Closed: never are removed from the daily hour entirely, and their "Why them" phrase goes into a short note on what the profile got wrong.

Then the process repeats with the next 100 accounts, and the weekly line tells you whether the next hundred should look like the last hundred or different. If the sheet shows meetings arriving on schedule and your hour is the limit, the fastest next step is not a hire but handing steps 3 to 5 to software: [see which buyers on LinkedIn match your profile right now](/try-ai), with no account needed, and start the next hundred from a ranked list instead of a blank search box. ## FAQ

### How many prospects should a startup contact per week?
As many as show a signal that week on a 100-account list, which in practice is 10 to 30, plus the handful that reach the four-week no-signal mark. Keep LinkedIn under 15 connection requests a day on a founder's own profile. Volume above what you can read and reply to by hand is wasted at this stage.

### Should a founder run outbound or hire an SDR first?
The founder first, for at least four weeks and usually a quarter. A hire needs a profile, an opener and a stage table that have stopped changing, and only the founder can produce those from real replies. Hire when the weekly line shows steady meetings and your own hour is the cap, not before.

### How long does an outbound sales process take to work at a startup?
Expect the first replies in week two, because they arrive on the second and third touch, and the first meetings in weeks two to four. Judge the process on four consecutive weekly lines, not on the first week, and change one variable at a time so you know what moved the numbers.

### Email or LinkedIn first for startup outbound?
Both on day zero, with the same signal in each. LinkedIn carries the context (they can see who you are) and email carries the detail and the thread. If you must pick one, pick the channel where your profile's signals appear, which for most B2B startups is LinkedIn, and keep email for the follow-ups.

### Do we need a CRM on day one?
No. One sheet with the stage table from step 7 does everything a CRM does for 100 accounts, and it forces you to define the stages before software hides the gaps. Move to a CRM when a second person needs the same view, or when the list passes a few hundred rows and the sheet slows you down.

### What reply rate should a startup expect from outbound?
Your own week-four number is the only one worth planning on. Published benchmarks vary widely and are mostly unsourced, and they mix large teams on bought lists with founders on hand-built ones. Track replies per touch 1 sent, per week, and aim to beat your own previous week rather than a figure from a vendor's blog.
