**A buying committee in B2B sales is the group of people inside one company who decide a purchase together: the person who feels the problem, the person who pays to fix it, the people who will use it, and anyone who can stop it.** That is the short answer to "what is a buying committee in B2B sales". It is rarely formal: it is whoever gets asked "are you sure?" before a contract is signed. At a 15-person company that is often one founder; at an enterprise, a dozen people.

## Why does the buying committee matter?

It matters because the deal is decided in conversations you are not in, and each person in those conversations is asking a different question. A seller who only talks to the person who answers emails has answered one question out of four, and the unanswered ones surface as "we decided to hold off".

The research points the same way from two directions. Gartner surveyed 632 B2B buyers in August and September 2024 and found that 74% of buyer teams showed what it calls unhealthy conflict: conflicting goals, disagreement on next steps, or being overruled by someone outside the group; groups that did reach consensus were 2.5 times more likely to call their deal high-quality ([Gartner, May 2025](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2025-05-07-gartner-sales-survey-finds-74-percent-of-b2b-buyer-teams-demonstrate-unhealthy-conflict-during-the-decision-process)). On the seller's side, Gong's analysis of 1.8 million deals closed in 2024 found that 77% involved more than one buyer contact, that won deals had twice as many buyer contacts as lost ones, and that in deals over $50K working several contacts at once lifted win rates by an average of 130% ([Gong Labs, 2025, updated May 2026](https://www.gong.io/resources/labs/data-shows-top-reps-dont-just-sell-they-orchestrate-with-ai/)). The committee argues among itself; a seller who knows who is in the room can help it agree.

A hypothetical example. Marrow Analytics, a 40-person data consultancy, is evaluating a tool that finds buyers on LinkedIn. Dana, the head of sales, started the search because reply rates fell. The founder owns the budget and will ask what it replaces. The one SDR will use it daily and will ask whether it is more work than the current spreadsheet. The operations lead who runs HubSpot will ask whether the data syncs. That is a four-person buying committee with three different questions, and only Dana is talking to vendors.

## Who is on a buying committee? Six roles and what each one asks

A buying committee is made of roles, not job titles, and one person often holds two or three. The table gives the six roles that appear in nearly every B2B purchase, the question each is silently asking, and the public trace on LinkedIn by which you can spot the person before the first call.

| Role | Typical title at a company of 50 to 500 people | The question they are silently asking | Public trace on LinkedIn |
|---|---|---|---|
| Problem owner (often the champion) | Head of sales, marketing lead, ops manager | "Will this make my problem go away before I get blamed for it?" | Posts or comments about the problem in their own words, asks peers what they use |
| Economic buyer | Founder, CEO, VP, budget-holding director | "What does this replace, and what happens if we wait a quarter?" | Posts about priorities, hiring, results; engages with peers rather than tools |
| Daily user | SDR, account executive, analyst, coordinator | "Is this more work for me, or less?" | Comments on tool threads, asks tactical how-to questions |
| Technical or operations gate | RevOps, IT, security, whoever owns the CRM | "Will it break what we already run, and is the data safe?" | Posts about integrations, data hygiene, stack changes |
| Finance, legal or procurement | Finance lead, office manager, counsel at larger firms | "Is the contract and the cost defensible?" | Rarely visible; found through the People tab, not through posts |
| Outside voice | Fractional leader, agency, investor, peer founder | "Would I recommend this to someone I advise?" | Comments on the problem owner's posts, listed as advisor or fractional on the profile |

Two notes on the table. At a company under 50 people the top three rows are frequently the same person: when we ran our own LinkedIn signals and AI SDR for two weeks and booked 14 qualified demos, the people who replied were founders complaining about cold outreach burnout, so the person who felt the problem was also the person who could pay for the fix and would use it. And the technical and finance gates rarely want your product; they want to not be surprised by it, which is why sellers who map a committee well bring them in early rather than meeting them as an objection in week eight. The lesson on [what a champion in sales is](/learn/what-is-a-champion-in-sales) covers the problem owner's row in detail, including the test that separates a champion from a friendly contact.

## How many people are on a buying committee in B2B sales?

Fewer than the headline figures suggest, unless you sell to large enterprises. The widely quoted numbers come from enterprise samples: Forrester's 2026 State of Business Buying report puts the typical decision at 13 internal stakeholders plus nine external influencers, rising for more complex purchases, and says procurement are decision makers in 53% of buying cycles ([Forrester, January 2026](https://www.forrester.com/press-newsroom/forrester-2026-the-state-of-business-buying/)). Gartner's survey found buying groups ranging from five to 16 people across as many as four functions ([Gartner, May 2025](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2025-05-07-gartner-sales-survey-finds-74-percent-of-b2b-buyer-teams-demonstrate-unhealthy-conflict-during-the-decision-process)). Gong's deal data shows the size tracks the money: large strategic deals in its sample averaged 17 buyer contacts ([Gong Labs](https://www.gong.io/resources/labs/data-shows-top-reps-dont-just-sell-they-orchestrate-with-ai/)).

The picture changes when the buyer is small. In a 2026 survey of 50 enterprise and mid-market buyers run by Influ2, a person-based advertising vendor, so a small and interested sample, half of the respondents said their buying group had two to four people, 42% said five to nine, and every respondent with a group of ten or more worked at a company of more than 1,000 employees ([Influ2, March 2026](https://www.influ2.com/academy/buying-committees)). Committee size follows the buyer's headcount and the size of the cheque more than it follows the industry.

So a working rule, to be replaced by your own numbers once you have looked at who was involved in your last ten won deals: selling to companies under 50 people, expect one to three people and plan to meet them all in the first two conversations; selling to companies of 50 to 500, expect a problem owner, a budget owner, a user and one gate, and ask in the first call who else will weigh in; above that, assume a committee you cannot see all of, and work with the problem owner to list it.

## Buying committee vs buying group vs decision maker: what is the difference?

The terms overlap, and vendors use them loosely. One table.

| Term | What it means | How it differs from a buying committee |
|---|---|---|
| Buying committee | Everyone who takes part in deciding one purchase | The term itself |
| Buying group | The same people, in Forrester's and many marketing teams' vocabulary; also a CRM object tying them to one opportunity | Same people; "buying group" is the marketing and RevOps word, "buying committee" the sales word |
| Decision maker | A person with authority over part or all of the decision | One role on the committee; there are usually several, each over their own piece |
| Economic buyer | The one decision maker who controls the budget and can say yes alone | The seat the deal cannot close without |
| Champion | A committee member who wants your deal and sells it internally | A role you hope someone takes; nobody is a champion by title |
| Account | The company you are selling to | The container; the committee is the people inside it who matter for this deal |
| Buyer persona | A description of a typical role, built from past deals | The template; the committee is the real people who fill it at one account |

The practical point: a buying committee is specific to one purchase. The same company convenes a different committee for a CRM than for a coffee supplier, and the founder who was the economic buyer last year may have delegated that role this year.

## How do you map a buying committee before the first call?

Start from the problem owner, because that is the person who leaves traces in public, and work outward. Here is a seven-line map that fits in a CRM note or a spreadsheet row. Fill it in for every account past the first conversation.

```
Account:            <company>, <headcount>, <why now>
Problem owner:      <name, title>  said in public: "<quote or topic>"
Budget owner:       <name, title>  distance from problem owner: same person / direct manager / another function
Daily users:        <names or team>
Can stop it:        <RevOps / IT / finance / legal>  met yet? yes / no
Outside voices:     <advisor, agency, peer>
Still unknown:      <which line above is a guess>
```

Four steps fill it.

1. **Find the problem owner by what they say.** Search the LinkedIn Posts tab for the problem in the buyer's words, such as `"reply rates" "outbound" "anyone else"` or `"we switched from" <tool you replace>`, sort by Latest, and read the comments as carefully as the posts, because the people who agree in the comments are problem owners at other companies.
2. **Find the budget owner by title** on the company's People tab, and note how far they sit from the problem owner: one reporting line away is a short deal, two functions away is a long one.
3. **List the daily users** from the same People tab.
4. **Find the gates.** Whoever lists HubSpot, Salesforce, security or finance in their headline will ask the integration and contract questions, so note them now and give the problem owner something to forward to them early.

Done by hand, the first step is the slow one: a daily search and a spreadsheet. Intentsly watches LinkedIn keyword posts and the people engaging with competitor and industry posts, then scores each person against your ideal customer profile, so the problem-owner line often arrives filled in, while the budget owner and the gates stay a People-tab lookup, since [LinkedIn signals](/features/linkedin-signals) are the only source it monitors. The [guide to finding decision makers on LinkedIn](/guides/find-decision-makers-on-linkedin) has the exact filters for steps two and four, and the lesson on [account-based prospecting](/learn/what-is-account-based-prospecting) covers working the whole list at once rather than one lead at a time.

The "still unknown" line is the most useful one. A deal where it reads "budget owner" is a deal you are forecasting on hope.

## How to use it this week

Three actions, each under an hour.

1. **Reconstruct the committee on your last five won deals.** Open each one, list every person who touched it, and sort them into the six roles. The pattern you see is your real buyer persona set, and it tells you how many people to expect next time. If every won deal had a RevOps person in it, your map needs that line from day one.
2. **Fill in the seven-line map for your three most active open deals.** Be honest in the "still unknown" line. For each unknown, write one ask you can make of the problem owner this week: "Who will look at the contract?" is easier to answer than "Who is the decision maker?"
3. **Find one problem owner at a target account before anyone there knows you.** Run the two search strings above for a company on your list, read what the person wrote, and open with that sentence rather than with your product. The lesson on [what a buying signal is](/learn/what-is-a-buying-signal) explains why a comment with an opinion is worth more than a profile view.

## FAQ

### What is a buying committee in B2B sales in simple terms?

It is the set of people at one company who together decide whether to buy something: the person with the problem, the person with the budget, the people who will use the product, and anyone who can block the purchase, such as IT, finance or legal. It is usually informal. Nobody calls a meeting; the decision is made in a chain of short conversations.

### How many people are on a B2B buying committee?

It depends on the buyer's size and the size of the purchase. Gartner's survey of B2B buyers found groups of five to 16 people ([Gartner](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2025-05-07-gartner-sales-survey-finds-74-percent-of-b2b-buyer-teams-demonstrate-unhealthy-conflict-during-the-decision-process)), and Forrester's 2026 report puts the typical decision at 13 internal stakeholders ([Forrester](https://www.forrester.com/press-newsroom/forrester-2026-the-state-of-business-buying/)). At companies under 50 people the committee is often one to three people, and the founder frequently holds several roles at once.

### What is the difference between a buying committee and a buying group?

In practice none. "Buying group" is the term Forrester and many marketing and RevOps teams use, and it is also the name of the CRM object some platforms use to tie several contacts to one opportunity. "Buying committee" is more common among salespeople. Both mean the people inside one account who take part in one purchase decision.

### Who is the most important person on a buying committee?

The economic buyer, because the deal cannot close without their yes, and the champion, because without one nobody argues for you in the meetings you are not in. In a small company these are often the same person. In a larger one, the most common mistake is spending all your time with a friendly user and never meeting the person who controls the budget.

### Does a bigger buying committee make a deal harder to win?

A bigger committee has more people who can disagree, and Gartner found unhealthy conflict in most buyer teams it surveyed ([Gartner](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2025-05-07-gartner-sales-survey-finds-74-percent-of-b2b-buyer-teams-demonstrate-unhealthy-conflict-during-the-decision-process)). But sellers who engage more of the committee win more often than those who rely on one contact. The risk lies less in the size than in how many members you have never spoken to.

### How do you find the buying committee at a company?

Start with the person who talks about the problem in public, on LinkedIn posts and comments, because that is usually the problem owner. Then open the company's People tab and find the budget owner by title, the likely users by team, and the gates by headline: whoever mentions the CRM, security or finance. Confirm the list in the first call by asking who else will weigh in.
