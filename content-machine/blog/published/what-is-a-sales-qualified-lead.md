> [!SUMMARY]
> - A sales qualified lead (SQL) is a lead a salesperson has checked and agreed is worth a sales conversation now: right fit, a real need, able to buy, and a reason to act.
> - Vendors use three different tests for "qualified": a rep says so, a meeting is booked, or a framework such as BANT is passed. Pick one and write it down.
> - Benchmarks put MQL to SQL conversion in the low teens for B2B, so most of what marketing hands over is not sales qualified.
> - If no marketing funnel feeds you, qualify from public evidence: posts, job ads, comments and the person's own words on LinkedIn.

**A sales qualified lead is a prospect that a salesperson has reviewed and judged ready for a direct sales conversation, because the person fits the ideal customer, has a problem you solve, can make or influence the purchase, and has a reason to act soon.** That is the short answer to "what is a sales qualified lead". The acronym is SQL, which has nothing to do with the database language. The word that matters is qualified: somebody checked, and the lead passed.

## Why does the sales qualified lead matter?

Because the label decides where selling time goes. Every lead that reaches a salesperson unqualified costs a call, a follow-up and a slot in the pipeline that a real buyer could have used, and the same lead marked "qualified" too early produces a forecast that will not close.

The share of leads that survive the check is small. First Page Sage's 2026 report, built on client data gathered from 2019 to November 2026 across 30 industries, puts the average MQL to SQL conversion rate at 16.1%, with a median of 15%; B2B SaaS sits at 13%, IT and managed services at 13%, and staffing and recruiting at 12% ([First Page Sage, MQL to SQL conversion rate by industry](https://firstpagesage.com/reports/mql-to-sql-conversion-rate-by-industry/)). Read the other way, roughly five in six marketing leads are not sales qualified when sales first looks at them.

Speed matters too, because qualification is partly a judgement about timing. HubSpot's write-up of an InsideSales audit of online leads reports a median first response time of three hours and eight minutes, an average of about 61 hours, and 47% of companies that never responded at all ([HubSpot, average lead response time](https://blog.hubspot.com/sales/average-lead-response-time-sales-data)). A lead that was sales qualified on Monday is often somebody else's customer by Thursday.

> [!EXAMPLE]
> A labelled, hypothetical example. A six-person Webflow agency gets 40 inbound form fills a month. The founder calls all 40 and closes two. She writes a sales qualified lead test: a company with 10 to 200 staff, a website project named in the message, a person who signs off or reports to the one who does, and a date they care about. Nine of the next 40 pass. She calls nine, closes two, and gets a week back.

## How does a lead become sales qualified?

Three different tests hide behind the same term, and the ranking pages mix them. Decide which one your team uses, because the number you track changes with it.

| Test | Who uses it | What "qualified" means | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| The rep says so | [Salesforce's definition](https://www.salesforce.com/blog/sales/sales-qualified-lead/): a lead flagged by a sales team member as likely to convert | A person with context made a call | Varies by rep; hard to compare month to month |
| A meeting is booked | [First Page Sage](https://firstpagesage.com/reports/mql-to-sql-conversion-rate-by-industry/) counts an SQL as an MQL that booked a meeting with a salesperson; [LinkedIn's glossary](https://business.linkedin.com/sales-solutions/resources/sales-terms/sales-qualified-lead) uses meeting acceptance as the outbound qualifying event | A visible event in the calendar | Counts anyone who accepts a call |
| A framework is passed | [HubSpot](https://blog.hubspot.com/sales/sales-qualified-lead) and most vendors: fits the persona, shows buying signals, has authority, budget and need (BANT) | Written questions answered yes | Buyers rarely volunteer budget in a first exchange |

Whichever test you pick, the inputs are the same four facts. The lesson on [lead qualification](/learn/what-is-lead-qualification) reduces the frameworks (BANT, CHAMP, MEDDIC) to three questions; this is the version that produces a pass or fail on a single lead.

1. **Fit.** The company and the person match your [ideal customer profile](/learn/what-is-an-ideal-customer-profile): size, industry, role. A student, a journalist or a competitor's employee fails here however keen they sound.
2. **Need.** They have named a problem you solve, in their own words. "Looking for a Webflow developer" is a need. "Downloaded your pricing guide" is interest, which belongs to the marketing qualified lead.
3. **Authority.** They decide, or they sit inside the group that decides. Gartner puts the typical buying group for a complex B2B purchase at six to 10 decision makers, so "authority" usually means one of the people in that room rather than the signer ([Gartner, the B2B buying journey](https://www.gartner.com/en/sales/insights/b2b-buying-journey)). The lesson on the [buying committee](/learn/what-is-a-buying-committee-in-b2b-sales) covers the roles.
4. **Timing.** Something makes this quarter different from the last one: a hire, a launch, a contract ending, a tool being replaced. Without a reason to act, a lead can pass the first three lines and still sit in the pipeline for a year.

A lead that passes all four is sales qualified under any of the three tests. A lead that passes fit and need but not timing is a warm lead to nurture, which the lesson on [what a warm lead is](/learn/what-is-a-warm-lead) separates from the rest.

### What if nobody is handing you MQLs?

Every page that ranks for this term assumes a marketing team feeding a sales team. A founder running outbound alone has the same decision to make with less data: is this stranger worth a message today? The four lines still work; the evidence comes from public behaviour instead of a form.

| Line | Form-fill evidence | Public LinkedIn evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Fit | Company field, job title field | Profile headline, company page size, industry |
| Need | "Tell us about your project" box | A post asking for a vendor, a comment complaining about a tool, a job ad for the role you replace |
| Authority | Title picked from a dropdown | Title plus who they tag or report to in posts; whether they write about budgets and outcomes |
| Timing | "When do you want to start?" | The date on the post, a funding announcement, a product launch, a "starting a new position" update |

This is where the manual work piles up: reading profiles, checking posts, grading each one by hand. ## Sales qualified lead vs MQL, SAL, PQL and buying signal

These five terms describe one lead at different moments, judged by different people.

| | Who judges | Evidence | Typical moment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Marketing qualified lead (MQL) | Marketing, usually by score | Downloads, visits, email clicks | After a form fill |
| Sales accepted lead (SAL) | Sales, on paper | A rep agrees the MQL meets the handoff rule and will work it | Before any contact |
| Sales qualified lead (SQL) | Sales, after contact | Fit, need, authority and timing confirmed in a conversation | After the first exchange |
| Product qualified lead (PQL) | Product analytics | A usage threshold crossed in a trial or free plan | After sign-up |
| Buying signal | Nobody yet; it is raw evidence | A public action that suggests need or timing | Before any contact |

The order is not fixed. In an inbound funnel a lead runs MQL, SAL, SQL. In outbound, a [buying signal](/learn/what-is-a-buying-signal) is often the first thing you see, and the lead goes from stranger to SQL in one conversation without ever being an MQL.

In a product-led company the [product qualified lead](/learn/what-is-a-product-qualified-lead) replaces the MQL. The SQL is the one stage every model shares, because every model eventually needs a person to agree that a conversation is worth having.

> [!NOTE]
> The sales accepted lead exists to stop arguments. Marketing says "we sent 200 leads", sales says "none were any good". An SAL step forces sales to accept or reject each lead against a written rule within a day, so both sides count the same number. If you are one person doing both jobs, skip it.

## How to use the sales qualified lead this week

1. **Write your test in four lines.** Fit, need, authority, timing, one sentence each, with the evidence that satisfies it. Put it where the whole team can see it. If you cannot agree on timing, use "named a date or an event within 90 days" as the first version. The guide on [qualifying leads before outreach](/guides/qualify-leads-before-outreach) turns this into a five-minute check per lead.
2. **Re-grade your current pipeline against it.** Go through every open deal and mark which lines pass on evidence rather than hope. Anything with two lines or fewer goes back to nurture. Teams that do this usually find the pipeline shrinks and the forecast gets more honest in the same afternoon.
3. **Count one ratio for a month.** Leads reviewed, leads qualified. Whether that is MQL to SQL or signals to SQL depends on where your leads come from, but the ratio tells you whether the people arriving are the people you want. If it sits far below the low-teens figures in the benchmark report ([First Page Sage](https://firstpagesage.com/reports/mql-to-sql-conversion-rate-by-industry/)), the problem is upstream: the wrong people are arriving, and the fix is targeting, not more calls.

## FAQ

### What is the difference between an MQL and an SQL?

A marketing qualified lead has engaged with marketing, usually by downloading, visiting or clicking enough to cross a score threshold, and marketing judges it. A sales qualified lead has been checked by a salesperson, usually in a conversation, and confirmed to fit, have a need, hold some authority and have a reason to act. The MQL shows interest; the SQL shows a buyer.

### What comes first, MQL or SQL?

In an inbound funnel the MQL comes first, then sales accepts it and qualifies it into an SQL. In outbound selling the order breaks: a prospect found through a public buying signal can go straight to a qualifying conversation and become an SQL without ever being an MQL. The SQL stage is the one that every model shares.

### What is a good MQL to SQL conversion rate?

First Page Sage's 2026 report, based on its client data from 2019 to November 2026, gives a 30-industry average of 16.1% and a median of 15%, with B2B SaaS at 13% ([First Page Sage, 2026 report](https://firstpagesage.com/reports/mql-to-sql-conversion-rate-by-industry/)). Treat these as a range from one agency's clients, not a target. A rate far above them often means the MQL bar is too high; far below, the wrong people are arriving.

### Who decides when a lead is sales qualified?

Sales does, in every common definition, which is the point of the term. Marketing can score, route and nurture, but the "qualified" label means a salesperson looked at fit, need, authority and timing and agreed the lead is worth a conversation now. In a one-person company that is the founder, applying a written test rather than a feeling.

### What is a sales accepted lead?

A sales accepted lead (SAL) is a marketing qualified lead that a salesperson has agreed, against a written handoff rule, to work. It sits between MQL and SQL and exists so marketing and sales count the same leads: sales must accept or reject each one, usually within a day, before any qualifying conversation happens. Teams where one person does both jobs can skip the stage.

### How long does it take to move from MQL to SQL?

It depends on the sales cycle, and the pages that rank for this question give ranges without a source, so treat any number with care. Measure it yourself instead: record the date each lead became an MQL and the date it was qualified, then look at the median after a month. If leads sit for weeks before anyone looks, speed of first response is the first thing to fix.
