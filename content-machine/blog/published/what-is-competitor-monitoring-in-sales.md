> [!SUMMARY]
> - Competitor monitoring in sales is watching what your competitors and their audiences do in public, so you can reach a buyer at the moment they start comparing or complaining.
> - For a seller the output is a named person and a reason to message them today, not a report about the competitor.
> - Five competitor signals are visible on LinkedIn without any tool: post engagers, new followers, public complaints, pricing or product changes, and switching hints in posts.
> - Monitoring tells you something changed; competitive intelligence tells you what it means; competitor analysis is the snapshot you do once a quarter.
> - Start with two competitors, a 20-minute morning check and a same-day reply rule.

**Competitor monitoring in sales is the practice of watching a competitor's public activity, and the public activity of the people who follow and engage with them, so you can reach a buyer at the moment they start comparing, complaining or switching.** That is the short answer to "what is competitor monitoring in sales". Pricing teams monitor competitors to adjust prices and marketing teams do it to adjust messaging. A sales team does it to find one person who is in the market this week, and to know what to say.

## Why does competitor monitoring matter in sales?

Competitor monitoring matters because most deals now have a competitor in them, and the buyer has usually picked a favourite before any seller hears from them. In Crayon's 2026 State of Competitive Intelligence survey of CI and revenue leaders, seven in ten teams said at least half of their sales opportunities are competitive ([Crayon, 2026](https://www.crayon.co/state-of-market-intelligence)). The same report found that only about 56% of teams share competitive intelligence with sales weekly or faster, and that teams which do report revenue impact at 79% against 41% for teams on a monthly-or-slower cadence.

The buyer's side is harsher. In 6sense's 2025 Buyer Experience Report, a study of more than 4,000 B2B buyers, 94% of buying groups had ranked their preferred vendors before first contact, and they bought from that preliminary favourite 77% of the time ([6sense via Business Wire, 12 November 2025](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20251112018032/en/)). If you are not on the shortlist when the buyer starts talking to vendors, monitoring your competitor's pricing page will not get you on it. Monitoring the people who follow and engage with your competitor can, because those people are building the shortlist in public.

A labelled example (hypothetical, not a customer): a competitor of yours publishes a post about their new reporting feature. Forty people react. One of them, an operations lead at a 60-person logistics company, comments that she has been asking for exactly this for a year. She is a user of your competitor with a documented frustration, she is active on LinkedIn this week, and she fits your profile. That comment is competitor monitoring in sales. The feature announcement itself is not.

## What should a sales team monitor about competitors?

A sales team monitors five things, and four of them are people rather than pages. The table below lists each signal, where to see it on LinkedIn by hand, what it tells you and the first move. The lesson on [buying signals](/learn/what-is-a-buying-signal) has the three-question test for deciding whether a match deserves a message at all.

| Signal | Where to see it on LinkedIn | What it means | First move |
|---|---|---|---|
| Post engagers | The reactions and comments list under a competitor's post | This person is in your category and paying attention this week | Engage with their own posts, then connect with a note about the topic, not the competitor |
| New followers | The competitor's company page followers (visible for your own page; for others, people who announce they joined, posted about or tagged the competitor) | They are evaluating or already using the competitor | Add to a watch list; message only when a second signal appears |
| Public complaint | A comment under the competitor's post, or a post that tags them, describing a missing feature or a bad experience | A switching window may be open | Message about the problem they named, never about the competitor |
| Pricing or product change | The competitor's own posts, their pricing page, their changelog | Their customers will be reading the small print this month | Prepare one honest comparison for the next conversation; do not mass-message |
| Switching hint | A post or comment saying "looking for an alternative to", "moving away from", "anyone using X instead of Y" | The buyer is building a shortlist today | Reply publicly with a real answer, including options you do not sell, then message |

Two rules keep the table useful. First, a signal names a person; "the competitor raised prices" is context until you can attach it to someone who will feel it. Second, never open with the competitor's name. Open with the problem the person described, because that is what they want solved.

> [!EXAMPLE]
> Connection note after a public complaint under a competitor's post (hypothetical): "Saw your comment about exporting reports by hand every Monday. We hit the same wall last year and wrote up how we got around it. Happy to send it over, no strings."

The engagers row is where most of the pipeline hides, and it is the easiest row to run by hand because every reaction and comment on a public post is visible to anyone with a LinkedIn account. The guide to [tracking competitor LinkedIn post engagers](/blog/track-competitor-linkedin-post-engagers) covers three ways to collect that list, and [how to monitor competitors on LinkedIn in 60 minutes a week](/guides/monitor-competitors-on-linkedin) turns it into a routine.

## How does competitor monitoring work for a sales team?

Competitor monitoring works by writing four decisions down once, then running them daily.

1. **Pick two or three competitors.** The ones your buyers name on calls, not the whole market. Crayon's 2026 data shows nearly eight in ten teams track 30 competitors or fewer, and a one-person sales team should track far fewer than that ([Crayon, 2026](https://www.crayon.co/state-of-market-intelligence)).
2. **List what counts.** For each competitor, which of the five signals you will act on, and what disqualifies a match: wrong role, wrong company size, a student, a vendor, a recruiter.
3. **Set the check.** Open each competitor's page and their last three posts every morning. Read the comments first, then the reactions. Twenty minutes for two competitors is realistic.
4. **Write the reply rule.** Who replies, how fast, and what the first message contains: a reference to what the person wrote, one useful thing, no link and no pitch.

When we launched Intentsly we ran a loop like this on LinkedIn ourselves, listening for founders complaining about cold outreach burnout, then let our own intent signals and AI SDR handle the outreach. We booked 14 qualified demos in 14 days with zero manual outreach, and the reply rate was 4x our previous cold campaigns, mostly from signals caught in real time (see [case studies](/case-studies)). The loop worked because the list was short and the reply went out the same day.

## Competitor monitoring vs competitive intelligence vs competitor analysis

These terms are swapped for each other on vendor pages, and they describe three different jobs with three different owners.

| | Competitor monitoring (sales) | Competitive intelligence | Competitor analysis | Battlecard |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Question it answers | Which of their buyers can I talk to this week? | What does this change mean for how we sell? | Where do we stand against them right now? | What do I say when they come up on a call? |
| Unit of work | One person and one moment | One finding and a recommendation | One snapshot of a competitor | One page per competitor |
| Cadence | Daily | Weekly, as the findings arrive | Quarterly or when something big happens | Updated when positioning changes |
| Typical owner | The seller or SDR | Product marketing or a CI lead | Product marketing or strategy | Sales enablement |
| Output | A message sent today | A Slack post, a brief, a battlecard update | A document with strengths, weaknesses, pricing | A card the rep reads mid-call |
| Fails when | Nobody replies the same day | It is shared monthly and nobody reads it | It is done once and never revisited | It disparages the competitor and the buyer notices |

Competitive intelligence vendors such as [Rivalize](https://rivalize.ai/competitive-intelligence-vs-competitor-monitoring) draw the line the same way: monitoring tells you that something changed, intelligence tells you what it means and what to do about it. Crayon's 2026 report adds the detail that matters for sellers: this year CI teams named internal data such as call recordings and employee knowledge as their top source at 54%, ahead of competitor websites at 48%, which means the people closest to buyers are already the best monitoring system most companies have ([Crayon, 2026](https://www.crayon.co/state-of-market-intelligence)). Crayon's own write-up calls sharing cadence one of the strongest predictors of revenue impact in the survey, ahead of tooling ([Crayon blog, 8 July 2026](https://www.crayon.co/blog/state-of-competitive-intelligence-2026)). The lessons on [social listening in sales](/learn/what-is-social-listening-in-sales) and [intent data](/learn/what-is-intent-data) cover the neighbouring terms this table leaves out.

## When is competitor monitoring the wrong tool?

Competitor monitoring is the wrong tool in three situations, and the vendor pages skip all three.

**You have no competitor with a public audience.** If your rivals do not post, have no company page activity and their customers never mention them, there is nothing to monitor. Watch [trigger events](/learn/what-is-a-trigger-event-in-sales) such as job changes, hiring and funding instead.

**You are tempted to mass-message a competitor's followers.** A follower list is a watch list, not a send list. LinkedIn limits accounts that send too many connection requests and messages; Intentsly caps every connected account at 30 messages and 30 connection requests a day and recommends 15, which is a sensible ceiling for a person doing this by hand. One relevant message to one person who complained beats fifty "I see you follow X" openers.

**You would rather criticise than help.** A buyer who follows your competitor likes something about them. A message that mocks that choice ends the conversation. The honest version names the problem the buyer described, offers one useful thing and lets the comparison happen later, on the buyer's terms.

> [!TIP]
> Reply to a public complaint within a day. A switching conversation that starts on Monday has its shortlist by Wednesday.

## How to use competitor monitoring this week

Three actions, each under an hour:

1. **Build the watch list.** Two competitors, their company pages and the authors of their last ten posts. Open the comments on each post and write down every person who fits your profile and said something specific. You will usually find three to eight names.
2. **Send five messages, one at a time.** Each one references what the person wrote, adds one useful thing and asks for nothing. Track the replies in a plain spreadsheet with the date, the signal and the outcome. The [LinkedIn message templates for buying signals](/blog/linkedin-message-templates-for-buying-signals) give a starting script per signal type.
3. **Run it by hand for a week before you automate.** If reading the comments takes longer than writing the replies, that is the step to hand off: the [LinkedIn signals feature](/features/linkedin-signals) watches competitor page followers and engagers, keyword posts and hashtags for you, and scores each person against your ideal customer profile so the morning starts at the reply.

## FAQ

### What is competitor monitoring in simple terms?
Competitor monitoring means keeping a regular eye on what your competitors do in public and how people respond to them. In sales, it means watching a competitor's posts, followers and the comments under their content for the moment one of their buyers shows frustration, starts comparing options or announces a change, and then starting a relevant conversation with that person.

### What is the difference between competitor monitoring and competitive intelligence?
Competitor monitoring is the collection step: it tells you that something changed, such as a new post, a price change or a public complaint. Competitive intelligence is the analysis step: it tells you what the change means and what to do about it, usually as a brief or a battlecard. A seller needs monitoring daily and intelligence weekly.

### What is the difference between competitor monitoring and competitor analysis?
Competitor analysis is a snapshot: a document that compares strengths, weaknesses, pricing and positioning at one point in time, usually revisited quarterly. Competitor monitoring is continuous: a daily check for changes and for people reacting to them. Analysis tells you where you stand; monitoring tells you who to talk to this week.

### Is competitor monitoring legal and ethical?
Reading public posts, public comments, public pricing pages and public company updates is ordinary research, and it is what every buyer does before a purchase. The lines not to cross are pretending to be a customer to get private information, scraping in ways a platform forbids, and sending messages that disparage the competitor. Monitor in public, message about the buyer's problem, and you stay on the right side of both.

### How often should a sales team monitor competitors?
Daily for the people signals, because a public complaint or a switching question has its answers within a day or two. Weekly for the competitor's own posts and pricing page, since those change more slowly. Twenty minutes every morning for two or three competitors is enough for a small team, and the habit matters more than the tool.

### Do you need a tool for competitor monitoring in sales?
No. Two competitors, their company pages, their last ten posts and a spreadsheet are enough to start, and you should run it by hand for a week to learn which signals produce replies. A tool earns its place when the watching takes longer than the replying, or when the list of competitors and engagers grows beyond what one person can check each morning.
