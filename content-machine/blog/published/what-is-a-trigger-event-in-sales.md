**A trigger event in sales is a change in a company's situation that gives it a new reason to buy, and it opens a limited window in which a seller who reaches out is more likely to be heard.** A new head of sales starts, a funding round closes, a big customer leaves, a tool is outgrown. The short answer to "what is a trigger event in sales" is this: a trigger event is the moment a company's reason to buy changes, not proof that anyone there is buying yet.

## Why does a trigger event matter?

A trigger event matters because it tells you when to contact a company, which is the part of outbound most lists leave out. A prospect list tells you who fits. A trigger event tells you which of those companies has a reason to listen this month.

Take a hypothetical example. Northgate Payroll, a 40-person software company, has used the same outbound tool for three years. Nobody there is shopping. Then the founder hires a first VP of Sales. Within weeks the new VP reviews every tool the team uses, because that is what new leaders do with inherited decisions. For the next quarter, Northgate is open to a conversation it would have ignored in any other quarter. The hire is the trigger event. The window is that quarter.

The reason the window matters so much is that buyers now do most of the work without you. In a Gartner survey of 646 B2B buyers fielded in late 2025, 67% said they prefer a rep-free buying experience, and Gartner notes that buyers search, vet suppliers and shape their needs "with fewer early interactions" ([Gartner, 9 March 2026](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2026-03-09-gartner-sales-survey-finds-67-percent-of-b2b-buyers-prefer-a-rep-free-experience)). A trigger event is your chance to be useful at the one stage where the same research still rates sellers highly: Gartner's follow-up release says reps "remain the most important information source when buyers are researching a business problem or need" ([Gartner, 20 May 2026](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2026-05-20-gartner-survey-finds-sixty-nine-percent-of-b-two-b-buyers-turn-to-sales-reps-to-validate-ai-generated-insights)). The trigger is what starts that research.

## What are the types of trigger events in sales?

The method has an origin. Craig Elias, who built a sales approach around trigger events in the 2000s, split them into three kinds in a [2008 CustomerThink article](https://customerthink.com/whats_big_deal_about_trigger_events/): a Bad Experience, a Transition and an Awareness event. Every item on the long lists that rank for this term fits one of the three.

| Type | What changed | Examples | Where you can see it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bad experience | Something the company relies on failed or disappointed | A vendor outage, a lost customer, a public complaint about a tool, a bad quarter | A LinkedIn post or comment complaining; a reply thread under a competitor's announcement |
| Transition | Who decides, or how the company is set up, changed | New executive, a champion moving to a new company, funding round, acquisition, new office, hiring wave | "I'm happy to share that I'm starting a new position" posts; "we raised" posts; job adverts |
| Awareness | The company learned something that makes the old way look expensive | New legislation, a competitor's launch, an analyst report, a conference talk, a viral post in their niche | Who liked and commented on the post that spread the idea |

The lists you will find elsewhere are longer. HubSpot's page runs to [29 types of trigger events](https://blog.hubspot.com/sales/types-of-trigger-events-and-how-to-track-them) and Autobound's guide to [60 signals in six categories](https://www.autobound.ai/guides/sales-trigger-events). Long lists are useful as a checklist for what exists. They are not a plan, because no small team can watch 60 things.

## Trigger event vs buying signal vs sales trigger

The three terms get swapped around, and the swap costs you deals, because each one asks for a different next step.

| | Trigger event | Buying signal | Sales trigger |
|---|---|---|---|
| What it is | A change in a company's situation | One action by a person that points at a purchase | A rule in a CRM or outreach tool that fires an action when a condition is met |
| Names | The company | The person, usually | Neither; it names a workflow step |
| Example | A funding round closed | The new VP asked "which outbound tools are people using?" in a post | "When a lead opens the email twice, create a task" |
| Tells you | There may now be a reason to buy | Someone is acting on a reason now | Nothing about the buyer; it is plumbing |
| Next step | Find the role that owns the change and start a conversation | Reply to that one thing, quoting it | Build it once, then watch what it fires |

The lesson on [what a buying signal is](/learn/what-is-a-buying-signal) goes deeper into the middle column. The short rule for this page: a trigger event is about the company and comes first; a buying signal is about a person and comes after; a sales trigger is a setting in your software. When someone searches for "sales trigger" they usually mean one of the first two.

## What does a trigger event not tell you?

A trigger event does not tell you who to talk to, and it does not tell you that anyone is buying. Those two gaps are where trigger-based outreach fails most often.

The first gap is the person. A funding announcement names the company and the investors. It does not say whether the founder, the new COO or a sales manager three levels down owns the problem you solve. Until you know that, you are messaging a logo. The lesson on [what an ideal customer profile is](/learn/what-is-an-ideal-customer-profile) covers the fit side; the [guide to finding decision makers on LinkedIn](/guides/find-decision-makers-on-linkedin) covers the person side.

The second gap is action. A new VP of Sales has a reason to review tools. Reviewing is not buying. Some new leaders keep every inherited tool for a year. A trigger event raises the odds; a buying signal from a named person is what confirms them. The strongest outreach quotes both: "You started in June and last week you asked about outbound tooling."

A third thing to know: the statistics that travel with this topic are weak. You will read that sellers who arrive first in the "window of dissatisfaction" close 74% of the time, attributed to Forrester via Craig Elias; the earliest version still reachable online is a [2013 InsideSales article](https://www.insidesales.com/leveraging-trigger-events/) that quotes the figure without a document. Vendor pages add reply-rate multiples measured on their own customers. Treat all of it as a direction, not a benchmark. The honest claim is smaller: a company whose situation just changed is more likely to take a meeting than one whose situation did not, and the number that matters is your own reply rate before and after you started using triggers.

## Which trigger events can a small team actually see?

A team of one to twenty people can watch two or three trigger events well, or sixty badly. Pick the ones that appear in public, on a channel you already read, for the roles you sell to.

Here is a one-minute rule. Write down your last five deals. For each, ask what changed at the company in the ninety days before the first call. If the same change appears twice, that is your first trigger. If nothing repeats, start with the Transition type, because job changes and hiring posts are the easiest to see without paying for a database.

On LinkedIn, the three most visible triggers for a small team are these:

1. **Job changes.** New leaders announce themselves. Search the Posts tab for `"happy to share" "new position" "VP of Sales"` and swap the title for the role that owns your problem.
2. **Hiring waves.** A company hiring its first two SDRs has decided to do outbound and has not yet decided how. Search `"we're hiring" SDR` or `"looking for" "sales development"` and filter by the company sizes you sell to.
3. **Public complaints.** The Bad Experience type shows up as comments. Search `"anyone else" "reply rates"` or the name of a tool you replace plus `"cancel"` or `"switching from"`.

Funding rounds, acquisitions and legislation are real triggers too, but every vendor's database surfaces them to every seller on the same morning, so your message lands in a crowd. The LinkedIn triggers above are noisier to find and quieter to compete on. Intentsly watches LinkedIn keyword posts and the people engaging with competitor and industry posts so that the Awareness and Bad Experience columns arrive as a scored list instead of a daily search; funding databases and job boards stay a manual check, since [LinkedIn signals](/features/linkedin-signals) are the only source it monitors.

## How to use trigger events this week

Three actions, each under an hour.

1. **Choose two triggers and write them down.** Use the five-deals rule above. Write one sentence per trigger that says what changed and which role you will contact when it does. Two triggers watched daily beat ten watched never.
2. **Run the searches once a day for five days.** Use the LinkedIn Posts tab with the strings above, sorted by Latest. Keep a plain list: company, person, trigger, date, link to the post. After five days you will know how many triggers a week your market produces, which decides whether you need a tool at all.
3. **Send one message per trigger that names the change.** The first line quotes the event in the prospect's own words, the second says why it usually creates the problem you solve, the third asks one question. No pitch, no deck. The free [Buying Signal Playbook](/signal-playbook) has a script for each of the twelve signals it covers.

## FAQ

### What is an example of a trigger event in sales?

A new VP of Sales joining a company is the classic example. The hire changes who decides and gives that person a reason to review every tool and process they inherited. Other common examples are a funding round, an acquisition, a public complaint about a current vendor, a hiring wave for a new team, and a new law that changes what the company must do.

### What is the difference between a trigger event and a buying signal?

A trigger event is a change in a company's situation, such as a new executive or a funding round, that may create a reason to buy. A buying signal is one action by a person, such as asking in public which tool to switch to, that shows someone is acting on that reason. The trigger comes first and names the company; the signal comes after and names the person.

### How long does a trigger event stay useful?

It depends on the type. A job change stays useful for roughly the new leader's first quarter, while they are still reviewing inherited decisions. A public complaint is useful for days, because the person wants an answer now. Funding and acquisitions fade over a few months as budgets get allocated. The rule is to act while the change is still the prospect's problem to solve, not their past.

### How do you find trigger events without paid tools?

Watch the public places where companies announce changes. On LinkedIn, the Posts tab search finds job-change announcements, hiring posts and complaints when you use exact phrases in quotes, such as "new position" with a job title. Company newsrooms, press releases and job adverts cover the rest. Pick two triggers and check them daily rather than trying to watch everything.

### Is a trigger event the same as a sales trigger?

Usually not, although people use the words loosely. A trigger event is something that happens at the prospect's company. A sales trigger, in most CRM and outreach tools, is an automation rule that fires an action when a condition is met, such as creating a task when a lead replies. One is about the buyer's world; the other is a setting in your own software.
