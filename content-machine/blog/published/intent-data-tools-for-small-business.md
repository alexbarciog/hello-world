Most intent data tools for small businesses are not the platforms you have heard of. Bombora, 6sense and Demandbase sell account-level topic scores on annual contracts without listed prices. A team of 1 to 200 people buys one signal at a time: who engages with competitors on LinkedIn, who visits your website, who compares you on G2, which target accounts change their pricing pages. The table below prices 8 tools from each vendor's own site on 30 October 2026, and the rule after it says which signal to buy first.

## What counts as intent data when you have under 200 employees?

Intent data is any record of a company or person doing something that suggests they are about to buy what you sell. The [definition and types of intent data](/learn/what-is-intent-data) cover first-party (behaviour on your own properties), second-party (a platform's own audience, such as G2 or LinkedIn) and third-party (publisher networks aggregated by a vendor such as Bombora).

For a small team the distinction that matters more is what the signal names. A website-visitor tool names a company. A LinkedIn engagement tool names a person and shows you the post they reacted to. A topic-score platform names a company and a topic, and leaves you to find the person. The closer the signal gets to a named person with a visible reason to talk, the less work is left before the first message, and the less you need a RevOps function to make the data useful.

Price works the same way. Topic-score platforms sell on contract value. Signal tools sell per seat, per identified company, per credit or per monitored page, which means a 5-person team can start under €100 a month and stop when it wants.

## Which intent signal should a small business buy first?

Pick by where your buyers already show themselves, not by which vendor has the longest topic list. The rule we use:

1. **Your buyers post and react on LinkedIn** (founders, sales and marketing leaders, agencies, consultants, most B2B SaaS under 200 people). Start with LinkedIn engagement signals. A person who comments on a competitor's post has named themselves, shown the topic and given you the opening line. This is where Intentsly fits, and the [12 LinkedIn buying signals worth chasing](/blog/linkedin-buying-signals) show what each one looks like.
2. **Your website gets real traffic from companies you could sell to** (a few hundred business visitors a month or more). Add a website-visitor tool. Start on Leadfeeder's free Lite plan to see whether the identified companies are ones you want; upgrade or switch to RB2B only if the list is good and you need names.
3. **You are listed in a G2 category and buyers compare you there.** G2 Buyer Intent is the only source for "who compared me with a competitor this week". It needs a paid brand package, so it is a second purchase, not a first.
4. **You sell to a known list of target accounts** and the trigger is something they change: a new pricing page, a job ad for the role you serve, a product launch. Visualping on the free plan covers 5 pages; the [weekly hiring-signal routine](/guides/track-hiring-signals-for-sales) covers job ads without any tool.
5. **You sell a category buyers research across many publisher sites**, with deal sizes that justify an annual contract and a marketing team to run ads against the data. Only then look at Bombora.

A sensible stack for a small team is one signal from the first two groups plus a free monitor from the fourth. Buying three paid intent sources before anyone has time to work the alerts is the easiest way to waste the budget.

## What each intent data tool does well, and where it is the wrong choice

### 1. Intentsly: LinkedIn engagement signals, scored and worked by an AI SDR

Intentsly watches LinkedIn only. You name the competitors and industry leaders whose audiences you want, plus keywords and hashtags, and it captures the people who like, comment on or share those posts, people posting about the topic, and the followers and engagers of competitor pages. Each person is scored against your ideal customer profile, enriched with a verified email, and the AI SDR opens the LinkedIn conversation, keeps it going and sends your Cal.com or Google Calendar link so the lead books the meeting. We ran it on our own launch and booked 14 qualified demos in 14 days with zero manual outreach, at a reply rate 4x our previous cold campaigns; most of those leads came from signals caught in real time.

Where it is the wrong choice: if your buyers are not active on LinkedIn, there is nothing for it to capture. It does not identify website visitors, track G2 comparisons or score publisher research. Outreach runs through up to 2 LinkedIn accounts per plan with a hard cap of 30 messages and 30 connection requests per account per day (15 is the recommended pace), so a team that wants to message 300 people a day from one seat should look elsewhere. HubSpot is the only CRM integration, and monitoring and outreach cover English, Romanian, German and Dutch. For a line-by-line view against a database tool, see [Intentsly vs Apollo](/compare/intentsly-vs-apollo).

### 2. Apollo.io: intent topics inside a contact database

[Apollo's own comparison page](https://www.apollo.io/insights/apollo-vs-6sense) describes "15K+ buying intent topics tied directly to contacts and accounts", with intent available from the Basic plan at $49 per user a month on annual billing and 6 intent topics on that tier; Professional is $79 and Organization $119 with a 3-user minimum, and the Free plan gives 900 credits a year. The strength is that the intent flag, the contact record and the email sequence live in one tool, so a 2-person team can go from "this account is researching our topic" to a sent email without exporting anything.

Where it is the wrong choice: a topic score tells you an account is reading about a subject, not who at the account cares or what they said, so the first message is still cold. Six topics on Basic is enough for one product line, not for a team selling several. If your outbound is LinkedIn-first rather than email-first, the intent data is attached to the wrong channel.

### 3. Leadfeeder (Dealfront): website visitors by company, free to start

[Leadfeeder's pricing page](https://www.leadfeeder.com/pricing/) shows a Lite plan that is "Free forever, no card needed" with the last 100 identified companies a month and 7 days of visitor history, then Discover from €79 a month billed annually (€113 monthly) for up to 50 identified companies, rising by tier to 1,000 companies. Activate, from €369 a month on annual billing, adds verified emails and phones and intent-signal filters. All paid plans carry a 14-day trial. For a small business this is the cheapest way to learn whether anyone worth selling to visits your site at all.

Where it is the wrong choice: a company name is not a lead. Someone still has to find the right person and write the message, and on Lite and Discover the tool does not give you a contact. Pricing is per identified company, so a site with real traffic grows into the upper Discover tiers quickly. Teams whose traffic is mostly from consumers, students or existing customers will see a list that looks busy and sells nothing.

### 4. RB2B: website visitors resolved to a person, mostly in the US

RB2B goes one step further than company identification. Its [help centre article on the Pro and Pro+ plans](https://intercom.help/retention-b2b/en/articles/8943609-plan-rb2b-pro), dated 1 May 2026, lists Pro at $149 a month for 600 credits, $249 for 1,250 and $349 for 2,500, with "contact-level identification primarily for U.S. visitors"; Pro+ adds international resolution. The article says rb2b.com takes precedence if the two differ, so check the site before buying. When it resolves a visitor, you get a person who looked at your pricing page, which is as warm as first-party intent gets.

Where it is the wrong choice: if most of your buyers are outside the US, the Pro plan will resolve few of them. Credits are consumed per identified person, so low-traffic sites pay for capacity they do not use. And a person who visited your pricing page once has not asked to be messaged; a reply that references the visit can feel like surveillance, so the outreach still needs a reason the prospect would accept.

### 5. G2 Buyer Intent: who is comparing you on G2

[G2's Buyer Intent page](https://sell.g2.com/data?trk=test) lists the signals as "category research, competitor comparisons, pricing page visits, and direct engagement with your profile", and calls it "verified, first-party intent data" with integrations into Salesforce, HubSpot, Marketo, 6sense, Demandbase and Outreach. [G2's external pricing guide](https://sell.g2.com/hubfs/External%20Pricing%20Guide%20PDF%20FY26-Q4.pdf?hsLang=en), marked updated August 2025, shows a Free brand package at $0, Starter at $2,999 for the first year and then $5,999 a year, and Professional and Enterprise at "Contact Sales", with Buyer Intent sold as an add-on to Professional and Enterprise only. If your buyers compare software on G2, nothing else tells you who compared you with a named competitor.

Where it is the wrong choice: the signal is company level, the add-on needs a Professional package whose price is not published, and a product with few reviews or no G2 category gets little traffic to read. Services businesses, agencies and consultancies are usually not on G2 at all.

### 6. Visualping: page changes on accounts you already target

Visualping is not sold as intent data, and that is why it is on the list. [Its business pricing page](https://visualping.io/business/pricing/) shows a free plan with 150 checks a month across 5 pages, Personal 1K at $14 a month or $120 a year for 10 pages, and Business 20K at $140 a month or $1,200 a year for 200 pages and 5 users. Point it at the pricing, careers and product pages of 20 target accounts and you get a message when something changes, which is a trigger event with a date on it. Competitor monitoring is named on the page as a use case.

Where it is the wrong choice: it watches pages, not people, so you must already know which accounts matter, and a change alert still needs a human to decide whether it means anything. It will never find a new account for you. For teams without a target list, it is a second tool, not a first.

### 7. LinkedIn Sales Navigator Buyer Intent: official, company level, on the Advanced plan

[LinkedIn's Sales Navigator page](https://business.linkedin.com/sales-solutions/sales-navigator-b) describes Buyer Intent as a feature that "shows you which companies are actively showing interest, so you can prioritize outreach where there's already momentum", and its footnote says "Available only on Advanced and Advanced Plus plans". [The compare-plans page](https://business.linkedin.com/sales-solutions/compare-plans) lists Core at US$119.99 a month or US$1,079.88 a year and Advanced at US$159.99 a month or US$1,799.88 a year per licence, with Advanced Plus priced on request, free trials on Core and Advanced for members who have not used a LinkedIn trial in the past 365 days, and prices marked as estimates that may exclude VAT. If you already pay for Sales Navigator, upgrading one seat to Advanced is the cheapest way to add an intent view.

Where it is the wrong choice: the signal is interest in your company, at company level, and it stays inside LinkedIn. It will not tell you who engaged with a competitor's post, and it does not send the message for you. [Intentsly vs LinkedIn Sales Navigator](/compare/intentsly-vs-linkedin-sales-navigator) walks through running both on one profile.

### 8. Bombora Company Surge: the enterprise standard, priced on request

[Bombora's intent page](https://bombora.com/intent/) describes a Data Co-op of "thousands of media destinations", says "86% of the data in our Data Co-op is shared exclusively with Bombora", monitors research across "25,300+ topics", and scores each account-topic pair by comparing the most recent three weeks of activity against a 12-week baseline, with a buying stage of early, mid or late. There is no pricing and no trial on the page; the call to action is "Speak to an expert". It is on this list so you know what the other seven are not trying to be: a map of which accounts in a whole market are researching a topic.

Where it is the wrong choice: for a small business, almost always as a first purchase. The output is an account and a topic, which still leaves finding the person, the channel and the message. Without a marketing team to run ads against the surge list and a sales team large enough to work hundreds of accounts, the data sits in a dashboard. If you want to see what topic-level intent does for your pipeline before talking to an expert, Apollo's intent topics on a per-seat plan are the cheaper way to find out.

## When is intent data the wrong buy for a small business?

Four situations where the money is better spent elsewhere:

- **Nobody owns the alerts.** Every tool above produces a daily list. If no one has 30 minutes a day to work it, the subscription buys nothing. Fix the routine first; the [7-step signal-based outbound set-up](/guides/set-up-signal-based-outbound) is the shortest version of that routine we know.
- **Your website gets under a few hundred business visits a month.** Website-visitor tools will identify a handful of companies, most of them vendors, recruiters and existing customers. Use a free plan to check, then stop.
- **Your market is tiny and known.** If you can list every account you could sell to, you do not need a tool to find in-market ones; you need page monitoring and a calendar reminder.
- **Your buyers do not research in public.** Some categories are bought on referral and renewal. There are no signals to buy; the intent data is in your customers' heads, and a referral programme beats every tool here.

## How to run intent data with no RevOps person

A weekly routine that fits one person and one tool, written as an example rather than a template you must copy:

1. Monday, 20 minutes: open the signal list, discard anything outside your ideal customer profile, and keep the 10 to 20 people or companies with a visible reason to talk (a comment on a competitor's post, a pricing page visit, a job ad). Scoring every prospect against your ideal customer profile before outreach is the manual step [Intentsly's LinkedIn signals](/features/linkedin-signals) do for you, with the signal, the person and a verified email together.
2. Tuesday to Thursday, 15 minutes a day: one message per person that names the signal in the first line, in plain words, with no pitch. The [Buying Signal Playbook](/signal-playbook) has a script for each of the 12 signals it covers.
3. Friday, 15 minutes: count replies by signal type. After four weeks, keep the signal that produced conversations and cancel the tool that did not.

That last step is the whole point of starting small. A platform sold on an annual contract cannot be cancelled after a bad month; a [$14 a month page monitor](https://visualping.io/business/pricing/) and a [free visitor plan](https://www.leadfeeder.com/pricing/) can.

Check what the LinkedIn signals look like for your own competitors before paying for anything: [/try-ai](/try-ai) shows matching buyers with no account, and the 7-day trial covers a full weekly cycle.

## FAQ

### How much do intent data tools cost for a small business?
From nothing to a few hundred a month, if you buy by signal. On 30 October 2026 the vendors' own sites showed Leadfeeder Lite and Visualping's base plan as free, [Visualping Personal 1K at $14 a month](https://visualping.io/business/pricing/), [Apollo Basic at $49 per user a month on annual billing](https://www.apollo.io/insights/apollo-vs-6sense), and [Leadfeeder Discover from €79 a month billed annually](https://www.leadfeeder.com/pricing/). Bombora and Advanced Plus Sales Navigator are priced on request.

### Can a small business afford intent data at all?
Yes, as long as you avoid the platforms that only sell annual contracts. Per-seat, per-company and per-page pricing lets a 2-person team start under €100 a month and cancel after a bad quarter. The real cost is the time to work the alerts, which is why one owned signal beats three subscribed ones.

### What is the difference between first-party and third-party intent data?
First-party intent data is behaviour on something you own: your website, your emails, your product. Third-party intent data is behaviour on other people's sites, aggregated by a vendor such as Bombora and sold as topic scores. Second-party sits between them: a platform's own audience, such as G2 comparisons or LinkedIn engagement, which the platform or a tool reading it can show you.

### Which intent data provider is best for small teams?
The one that names a person with a reason to talk in the channel you already use. For LinkedIn-first teams that is an engagement-signal tool such as Intentsly; for email-first teams with a database habit, Apollo; for sites with real business traffic, Leadfeeder's free plan first. There is no single best provider across all three situations.

### How long does it take to set up an intent data tool?
Signal tools take minutes to a day: connect a LinkedIn account or paste a tracking script, name competitors or pages, and wait for the first alerts. Platform contracts take longer because the value depends on integrations and ad activation. Give any tool a four-week trial window before judging it, because the first week's signals are often thin.

### Does intent data actually work for outbound?
It works when the signal names a person and the message refers to it. Our own launch, run entirely on LinkedIn intent signals with the AI SDR sending the messages, produced 14 qualified demos in 14 days with zero manual outreach, at a reply rate 4x our previous cold campaigns. The same data left in a dashboard, with cold templates sent to the account, produces cold-template results.
