**A sales trigger is an observable event that tells a seller now is the right moment to contact a specific company or person.** The word is used for three different things: a change at the prospect's company, an action by one person there, and a rule in your own software that fires when one of those happens. The short answer to "what is a sales trigger" is any of the three, which is why the lists you find online disagree with each other.

## What is a sales trigger in practice?

In practice a sales trigger is whichever of three events your team has agreed to act on. Each one answers a different question, and mixing them up is the most common reason trigger-based outreach disappoints.

| Meaning | What happened | Example | What it tells you |
|---|---|---|---|
| Company change | Something shifted at the prospect's company | A new VP of Sales started; a funding round closed; a competitor announced price rises | The company may have a new reason to buy |
| Person's action | One person did something that points at a purchase | A founder posted "which outbound tools are people using?" | Someone is acting on a reason now |
| Tool rule | A condition in your CRM or outreach tool was met | "When a lead replies, create a task and pause the sequence" | Nothing about the buyer; it moves work inside your own process |

Most ranking pages mean the first one. Yesware defines a sales trigger as any event that creates a good opportunity for a rep to reach out and lists ten of them, from job changes to trade-show appearances ([Yesware](https://www.yesware.com/blog/sales-triggers/)). Close's glossary stretches the word to the second meaning too, counting pricing-page visits and chatbot conversations as triggers alongside funding rounds ([Close](https://close.com/sales/glossary/triggers)). The third meaning is what a CRM's help centre means by the word. A working process uses all three.

## Why does a sales trigger matter?

A sales trigger matters because it decides when you contact someone, and timing is the part of outbound a prospect list cannot give you. A list says who fits. A trigger says which of those people has a reason to listen this week.

Take a hypothetical example. Harborline Logistics, a 60-person freight software company, has ignored every outbound tool pitch for two years. Then three things happen in one month: the company announces a Series A (company change), the new head of growth posts asking how other teams find leads on LinkedIn (person's action), and your tool creates a task when a saved search finds that post (tool rule). The message you send quotes the post, mentions the round in one clause and asks one question. In any other month it would have landed in a quiet inbox.

One first-hand example, with its limits stated. When Intentsly launched, we ran our own signal monitoring and AI SDR on LinkedIn and chose one trigger: founders posting complaints about cold outreach burnout, which is a person's action, not a company change. The result was 14 qualified demos in 14 days with zero manual outreach, a reply rate 4x our previous cold campaigns, and most of those leads came from signals caught in real time ([case study](/case-studies)). That is one company over two weeks, so it shows the trigger worked for us and says nothing about your market.

## How do the three kinds of sales trigger work together?

The three meanings form a chain. A company change creates the reason, a person's action shows someone acting on it, and a tool rule makes sure you see both without reading LinkedIn all day.

A company change on its own tells you a logo may be in the market, not which person owns the problem or whether anyone is doing anything about it; the lesson on [what a trigger event in sales is](/learn/what-is-a-trigger-event-in-sales) covers that gap. A person's action on its own tells you who is moving but not why; the lesson on [what a buying signal is](/learn/what-is-a-buying-signal) covers that side. The strongest first line uses both: "You started in September and last week you asked which outbound tools people trust."

The tool rule is the piece most lists leave out. If a job change sits in a spreadsheet until Friday, the window closes. CUFinder, one of the few ranking pages that separates triggers from buying signals and intent data, calls this trigger decay: each event stays useful for a different length of time, and the author reports their own windows rather than a cited study ([CUFinder](https://cufinder.io/blog/wiki/sales/sales-triggers/)). A trigger without a rule that surfaces it the same day is a trigger you will act on late.

## Which sales triggers are worth watching?

The triggers worth watching are the ones that name a person, stay open long enough to act on, and reach few other sellers on the same morning. Every list online treats a funding round and a public complaint as equals. They differ on four questions. Here is a scorecard of twelve common triggers graded on four questions, as a hypothetical planning tool rather than measured data.

| Trigger | Names a person? | Window stays open | Who else sees it the same day | Free to see on LinkedIn? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| New executive hired | Yes, the executive | About a quarter | Everyone with a job-change alert | Yes, the "new position" post |
| Champion moved to a new company | Yes, the champion | About a quarter | Few, if you tracked the person | Yes, the "new position" post |
| Funding round announced | No, the company | A few months | Every seller with a funding database | Yes, the "we raised" post, with a delay |
| Hiring wave for a new team | Sometimes, the hiring manager | Weeks to months | Many, through job boards | Yes, "we're hiring" posts and job adverts |
| Public complaint about a current tool | Yes, the complainer | Days | Few, because it is hard to search for | Yes, posts and comments |
| Public question asking for recommendations | Yes, the asker | Days | Few, same reason | Yes, posts and comments |
| Engagement with a competitor's post | Yes, each liker or commenter | Days to weeks | Few, unless the competitor is large | Yes, the post's reactions list |
| Merger or acquisition | No, two companies | Months | Every seller reading the news | Partly, through announcements |
| New product or feature launch at the prospect | Sometimes, the launcher | Weeks | Many, through the announcement | Yes, the launch post |
| New legislation in their industry | No, a whole industry | Months | Every seller in that industry | Partly, through discussion posts |
| Layoffs or a bad quarter | No, the company | Weeks | Everyone reading the news | Partly, through "open to work" posts |
| Speaking at or attending an event | Yes, the speaker | The weeks around the event | Many, through the agenda | Yes, the speaker's post |

Read the table by column: the first decides whether you can write a message at all, the second how fast your rule needs to fire, the third how crowded the inbox will be, the last whether you need a paid database.

Two patterns fall out. The crowded triggers (funding, M&A, legislation, layoffs) are easy to buy and hard to win on, because the alert reaches every competitor at the same time. The quiet triggers (complaints, questions, competitor-post engagement, a champion's move) are harder to find and much less contested. A team of one to twenty people usually does better watching two quiet triggers daily than ten crowded ones weekly. ## Sales trigger vs trigger event vs buying signal vs intent data

The four terms overlap, and vendors use them interchangeably on purpose. DealHub's glossary lists "buying signals, trigger events, trigger points" as synonyms for sales triggers ([DealHub](https://dealhub.io/glossary/sales-triggers/)). For a working team they are better kept apart.

| Term | Unit | Time | Best use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sales trigger | Any event you have agreed to act on | A moment | The umbrella word for your team's watch list |
| Trigger event | A change at a company | A moment, with a window after it | Deciding which companies to approach this quarter |
| Buying signal | An action by a person | A moment | Deciding who to message today and what to quote |
| Intent data | Many signals, stored and scored | A trend over weeks | Ranking accounts when you have more signals than hours |

If your team has one watch list, call its entries sales triggers and stop there. If you are storing signals and scoring accounts over time, you have moved into [intent data](/learn/what-is-intent-data), which is a different lesson.

A note on the statistics you will meet while reading about this. The win-rate figures attached to sales triggers travel without documents. Persana attributes a 74% win-rate lift to Forrester, an 8x purchase likelihood after funding to Crunchbase and a 10x figure for new executives to nobody ([Persana](https://persana.ai/blogs/sales-triggers)); Bounce Watch attributes a 35% to 50% first-vendor win rate to Forrester, a 70% figure to Harvard Business Review and a 3x to 4x reply-rate lift to HubSpot, each linked only to that publisher's homepage ([Bounce Watch](https://bouncewatch.com/blog/signal-intelligence/what-are-sales-trigger-events)); CUFinder quotes the same 74% and calls it secondhand. None of the nine pages opened for this lesson links those figures to a report you can read. Treat them as a direction, and measure your own reply rate before and after you start acting on triggers.

## How to use sales triggers this week

Three actions, each under an hour, that turn the definition into a routine.

1. **Write your trigger list in the three columns.** For each trigger you plan to watch, write what the company change is, what the person's action looks like, and what rule surfaces it (a saved LinkedIn search checked daily counts as a rule). If a row has only the first column, you have a research project, not a trigger. Start with two rows from the quiet half of the scorecard.
2. **Run the person's-action search for five days.** On LinkedIn, open the Posts tab, sort by Latest, and search exact phrases such as `"anyone recommend" "outbound"` or the name of a tool you replace plus `"switching"`. Keep a plain list with the person, the post, the date and which trigger it matched. After five days you know how many triggers a week your market produces, which tells you whether a daily manual check is enough. The [guide to tracking hiring signals](/guides/track-hiring-signals-for-sales) runs the same exercise for the hiring-wave row.
3. **Send one message per trigger that quotes the event.** First line quotes the post or the change in the prospect's own words, second line says why it usually creates the problem you solve, third line asks one question. No deck. When the daily check becomes a chore, the [guide to setting up signal-based outbound](/guides/set-up-signal-based-outbound) turns the list into a scored routine with a message per trigger.

## FAQ

### What is an example of a sales trigger?

A founder posting on LinkedIn to ask which outbound tool other teams use is a sales trigger of the person's-action kind. A new VP of Sales starting at a target company is a sales trigger of the company-change kind. A rule in your CRM that creates a task when a lead replies is a sales trigger of the tool kind. All three are called sales triggers, so say which one you mean.

### What is the difference between a sales trigger and a buying signal?

Sales trigger is the umbrella word for any event your team has agreed to act on. A buying signal is one kind of sales trigger: an action by a person that points at a purchase, such as asking for recommendations or commenting on a competitor's launch. A change at a company, such as a funding round, is also a sales trigger but is usually called a trigger event, not a buying signal.

### How soon should you act on a sales trigger?

Within the window the trigger stays open, which differs by type. A public complaint or a question stays useful for days, because the person wants an answer now. A new executive stays useful for roughly their first quarter. Funding rounds and acquisitions fade over months as budgets get allocated. The practical rule is a daily check for the fast triggers and a weekly check for the slow ones.

### Is a sales trigger the same as a CRM trigger?

Only in the third meaning. A CRM trigger is a rule inside your own software that fires an action when a condition is met, such as pausing a sequence when a lead replies. It is part of a trigger-based process because it is what surfaces a company change or a person's action in time, but it says nothing on its own about whether anyone is buying.

### Do sales triggers work without paid tools?

Yes for the LinkedIn-visible triggers in the scorecard: new-position posts, hiring posts, complaints, questions and the reactions on a competitor's post are all searchable for free. Funding and acquisition data is also public, just slower to find by hand. What a tool adds is speed and scoring, which matters once your market produces more triggers a week than you can read.
