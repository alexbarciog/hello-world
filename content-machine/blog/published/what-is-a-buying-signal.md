**A buying signal is one observable action, statement or event that shows a specific person or company is moving toward a purchase decision.** A founder commenting "we're replacing our CRM in January" under a LinkedIn post is a buying signal. So is a prospect asking about contract terms on a call. The short answer to "what is a buying signal" is a single piece of evidence about timing: not who might buy someday, but who is deciding now.

## Why does a buying signal matter?

Because buyers do most of their deciding without you in the room. In a Gartner survey of 646 B2B buyers run from August to September 2025, 67% said they prefer a rep-free experience and 45% said they used AI during a recent purchase ([Gartner, 9 March 2026](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2026-03-09-gartner-sales-survey-finds-67-percent-of-b2b-buyers-prefer-a-rep-free-experience)). A second release from the same survey says buyers used an average of seven information sources during a recent purchase ([Gartner, 20 May 2026](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2026-05-20-gartner-survey-finds-sixty-nine-percent-of-b-two-b-buyers-turn-to-sales-reps-to-validate-ai-generated-insights)). If most of the decision happens in places you cannot see, the visible moments are the only timing information you get.

A labelled example. Suppose a five-person agency sells HubSpot onboarding. Their cold list holds 400 marketing managers, and nothing on it says who has a problem this month. Then one of those managers posts on LinkedIn asking which CRM her team should move to. That post is the buying signal. The message to her can quote her own question; the message to the other 399 cannot. Same list, one sentence of evidence, and the order of the week's work changes.

## What are the types of buying signals?

Most explainers split buying signals into verbal, non-verbal and digital. That split describes where the signal appears, which is less useful than when it appears. For a small team, the division that matters is whether the signal happens before a conversation exists or inside one.

**Public signals (before any conversation).** A named person does something visible on an open platform: posts a question, complains about a tool, comments under a competitor's announcement, follows a competitor's company page, starts a new role, or their company announces funding or a hiring push. You did not have to be in the room. These signals give you a reason to start a conversation, and they are the only kind that helps a company with no pipeline yet.

**Conversation signals (inside a deal).** A prospect you are already talking to asks about pricing, implementation time or security reviews, brings a colleague from finance or legal into the thread, replies faster than before, or names an internal deadline. These are the signals most sales articles describe. They tell you a live deal is moving, and they are invisible until the deal exists.

A third group, **behaviour on your own website** such as pricing-page visits and trial sign-ups, is real and first-party, and only covers people who already found you.

### A three-question test

Lists of 40 example signals do not help when you see something new. Three questions do. An observation is a buying signal when you can answer yes to all three.

1. **Is it a specific action by a specific person or company?** "Marketing managers care about attribution" is a market fact. "Dana, head of marketing at a 60-person SaaS, asked for attribution tool recommendations yesterday" is a signal.
2. **Does it connect to the problem you solve?** A funding announcement is a signal for a recruiter and noise for a cybersecurity vendor, unless the round comes with a hiring plan that touches security.
3. **Did it happen recently enough to still be true?** A question asked yesterday is open. The same question asked in 2024 was answered long ago.

If any answer is no, you have context, not a signal. Context helps you write the message; it is not a reason to send one.

## Buying signal vs trigger event vs intent data

The three terms overlap and get used as synonyms. They are not the same thing, and the difference changes what you do next.

| | Buying signal | Trigger event | Intent data |
|---|---|---|---|
| What it is | One action by a person or company that points at a purchase | A change in a company's situation that often precedes buying | A collection of signals, stored and scored over time |
| Example | "Which CRM should we switch to?" asked in public | New VP of Sales hired; funding round closed | A score showing a company's research on "CRM migration" rose this month |
| Who it names | The person, usually | The company | The company, sometimes the person |
| How certain | High, you can read it yourself | Medium, the change may not lead to a purchase | Varies, depends on how the score was built |
| What you do | Reply to that one thing, quoting it | Reach out to the role that owns the change | Decide whom to contact first this week |

A short rule: a trigger event tells you a company may soon have a reason to buy; a buying signal tells you someone at that company is acting on the reason; intent data is what you get when you store enough signals to rank people. The lesson on [what intent data is](/learn/what-is-intent-data) covers the third column in detail.

## How strong is the signal?

Not every signal deserves a message today. Use this table to rank what you saw before you write anything. Copy it into a sheet and add a row whenever you meet a signal that is not on it.

| Signal | Strength | Why | What to do |
|---|---|---|---|
| Asks in public which tool to buy, in your category | Strong | Named person, stated need, open question | Answer the question the same day, in the thread, then follow up privately |
| Complains in public about a competitor | Strong | Named person, active pain, timing clear | Reply with what you would do about that specific complaint |
| Asks about pricing, contract or implementation inside a deal | Strong | Only people planning a purchase ask these | Answer fully and propose the next step with a date |
| Brings a colleague from finance, legal or IT into the thread | Strong | Internal buy-in is being built | Prepare the material that colleague will need |
| Starts a new role that owns your problem | Medium | Reason to buy, no stated need yet | Congratulate, then ask one question about their first-quarter plan |
| Company announces funding or a hiring push | Medium | Budget may exist, need unknown | Check whether the hires touch your problem before reaching out |
| Comments under a competitor's or industry leader's post | Medium | Interested in the topic, intent unknown | Read the comment; respond to its content, not to the fact of commenting |
| Follows a competitor's page or likes a post in your category | Weak | One tap, no stated need | Note it; wait for a second signal before messaging |
| Opens your email | Weak | Could be a preview pane | Ignore unless paired with a reply or a click |

Two weak signals from the same person in the same week are worth one medium signal. One strong signal is worth more than any number of weak ones.

## Where do single buying signals mislead?

Four ways, and each costs a small team time it does not have.

**The person is not the buyer.** An intern, a job seeker, a consultant researching for a client and a competitor's employee all ask the same public questions as a buyer. Check the profile before you check the message draft.

**The signal is stale.** A question from last year is still indexed by search engines and still looks like a question. Treat anything older than a few weeks as background.

**The signal is about the topic, not the purchase.** People comment under posts to be seen, to disagree, or to be polite to the author. A comment proves attention; the text of the comment proves or disproves intent. Read it.

**The signal is strong and the fit is wrong.** A perfectly phrased "which tool should we buy" from a company you would not sell to is still a no. Check the fit before the timing.

## How to use buying signals this week

Three actions, in order, each under an hour the first time.

1. **Write your signal list.** Pick the five actions that, in your experience, came before your last few deals. If you have no deals yet, start with the first four rows of the strength table. The free [Buying Signal Playbook](/signal-playbook) lists twelve signals worth chasing and eight to ignore, with the LinkedIn triggers and a script for each, if you want a tested list rather than a blank page.

2. **Watch one place by hand for five days.** Choose one competitor's LinkedIn page and one industry leader your buyers follow. Each morning, read the new comments and the new followers, run each through the three-question test, and write down the people who pass. By Friday you will have a short list and a feel for which signals are real in your market. 3. **Reply to the signal, not to the person's job title.** Open with the thing they did or said. We ran this on ourselves at launch: our own signals on LinkedIn, aimed at founders complaining about cold-outreach burnout, with the conversation handled by our AI SDR. The result was 14 qualified demos in 14 days with zero manual outreach, and a reply rate 4x our previous cold campaigns. The signal gave every message its first line.

## FAQ

### What is a buying signal in sales?

In sales, a buying signal is one observable action by a specific person or company that shows they are moving toward a purchase: asking which tool to buy, complaining about a current vendor, asking about pricing or implementation, or bringing a colleague into the conversation. Sellers use buying signals to decide whom to contact first and what the first sentence should say.

### What is the difference between a buying signal and buying intent?

Buying intent is the state of wanting to buy; a buying signal is the visible evidence of that state. You cannot observe intent directly, so you infer it from signals. Several signals from the same person, stored and scored over time, become intent data, which is a ranked view of who is most likely to be in-market now.

### What is the difference between a strong and a weak buying signal?

A strong signal names a person, states a need and is recent: a public question about which tool to buy, or a pricing question inside a deal. A weak signal shows attention without a stated need: a like, a follow, an email open. Act on one strong signal the same day; wait for a second weak signal before messaging.

### How quickly should you respond to a buying signal?

As soon as the signal is still true. A public question gets stale within days because other people answer it, so reply the same day. A new-role or funding signal stays useful for weeks, because the need builds over the first quarter. In every case, respond to the content of the signal, not to the fact that it happened.
