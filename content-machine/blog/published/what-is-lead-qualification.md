> [!SUMMARY]
> - Lead qualification is the decision whether a lead is worth your time: can the company buy, is the problem live now, and is this the person who owns it.
> - Every framework (BANT, CHAMP, FAINT, MEDDIC) asks those three questions in a different order; small teams need the three questions, not the acronym.
> - Two of the three can be answered from public behaviour before you send a message. Only one needs a conversation.
> - Disqualifying early is the point. A lead you drop in two minutes costs nothing; one you chase for a month costs the deals you did not work.

**Lead qualification is the process of deciding whether a lead is worth pursuing, by checking that the company can buy, that the problem you solve is live now, and that you are talking to the person who owns it.** That is the short answer to "what is lead qualification". The acronyms, checklists and MQL or SQL labels are ways of asking those three questions in order. A lead that passes gets your time this week; one that fails gets a polite no or a date to look again.

## Why does lead qualification matter?

Because selling time is the scarcest thing a small team has. In Salesforce's State of Sales research, a double-anonymous survey of 7,775 sales professionals run from 24 August to 30 September 2022, reps said they spend just 28% of their week actually selling ([Salesforce, State of Sales](https://www.salesforce.com/news/stories/sales-research-2023/)). Every call with a company that cannot buy comes out of that slice, and a founder doing sales part-time has an even smaller slice to protect.

The second reason is on the buyer's side. Gartner reports that 77% of B2B buyers say their latest purchase was very complex or difficult, and that customers spend around two-thirds of a buying journey gathering and processing information ([Gartner, Customer Confidence](https://www.gartner.com/en/sales/insights/customer-confidence)). A buyer in that state has no patience for a seller who has not checked whether the fit exists. Qualification is how you earn the right to the conversation.

A labelled example. Suppose a two-person agency that builds HubSpot setups has two new names. Lead A is the head of marketing at a 60-person software company who posted on LinkedIn yesterday asking which HubSpot partner people recommend. Lead B is a sales manager at a 3,000-person insurer who liked one of the agency's posts in March. Lead A passes all three questions from public information alone; Lead B fails on size and on timing, and nobody should spend a call finding that out.

## How does lead qualification work?

Every method reduces to three checks, done in this order because each one is cheaper than the next.

1. **Can this company buy?** Size, industry, geography and whether companies like it have paid for your category before. This is fit against your [ideal customer profile](/learn/what-is-an-ideal-customer-profile), and it is answerable from a company page in under a minute.
2. **Is the problem live now?** A hire, a public complaint, a question asked in your category, engagement with a competitor. This is timing, and it is what separates a lead from a name in a database. The lesson on [what a buying signal is](/learn/what-is-a-buying-signal) covers the evidence that counts.
3. **Is this the person who owns the problem?** The one who feels the pain, or the one who signs. In a company of 50 those are often the same person; in a company of 5,000 they rarely are, which is why [buying committees](/learn/what-is-a-buying-committee-in-b2b-sales) exist. This check usually needs a conversation.

The first two checks happen before anyone speaks. For an outbound team that is most of the work, because the question is which 20 of 200 strangers deserve a message. Intentsly's [LinkedIn signals](/features/linkedin-signals) run those two checks for you: everyone who engages with a monitored post, page or keyword is scored against your ideal customer profile and ranked before any message goes out, so the list you open is already sorted by fit and timing.

### What the frameworks add

The acronyms are question lists. BANT (Budget, Authority, Need, Timeline) is the oldest and the one most people learn first. CHAMP (Challenges, Authority, Money, Priorities) reorders it so the conversation starts with the buyer's problem, and MEDDICC adds Metrics, Decision criteria, Decision process, a Champion and Competition for enterprise purchases ([Salesforce, lead qualification](https://www.salesforce.com/uk/blog/lead-qualification/)). GPCTBA/C&I and ANUM are further variations on the same list ([PandaDoc, lead qualification](https://pandadoc.com/blog/lead-qualification)).

FAINT (Funds, Authority, Interest, Need, Timing) was written by RAIN Group to fix one BANT habit: "by leading with budget, BANT conditions sellers to disqualify leads who don't have money set aside." It asks instead whether the company has the means to act ([RAIN Group, How to qualify sales leads](https://www.rainsalestraining.com/blog/how-to-qualify-sales-leads)). For companies of one to 200 people, where few purchases have a budget line before the problem appears, that is the better question.

| Framework | Best for | Skip it when |
|---|---|---|
| BANT | Short sales cycles, buyers with a set budget | Your buyers find money after they find the problem |
| CHAMP | Warm leads, problem-first conversations | You need a one-minute screen, not a discovery call |
| FAINT | Small and mid-size buyers without budget lines | The purchase is a formal procurement |
| MEDDICC / GPCTBA | Enterprise deals with many stakeholders and a long process | The whole buying committee is one or two people |

The rule for a small team: if your deal closes in under a quarter with one or two people involved, use the three questions above and borrow FAINT's view of money. Pick up MEDDICC when you start losing deals you thought were won because someone you never met said no.

### A qualification card you can copy

> [!TIP]
> Write the answers down before the call, and treat every "unknown" as the first question to ask.

| Check | Pass | Fail | Where you find it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Company size inside your range | Yes | Far outside | LinkedIn company page |
| Industry or model you have sold to before | Yes | Never, and no reason to think it works | Company page, website |
| A live signal in the last 30 days | Asked a question, hired, complained, engaged with a competitor | Nothing since last quarter | LinkedIn posts, jobs, comments |
| The person owns the problem or signs | Title plus their own words | Student, intern, agency pitching you | Profile, the conversation |
| Means to act | Has paid for this category or an adjacent one | Says there is no money and no path to it | The conversation |

Two fails, or one fail on company size, and the lead is out for now. One fail on timing goes to a dated watch list, not the bin.

## Lead qualification vs lead scoring vs MQL and SQL

These terms get used as synonyms, and they are different tools.

| | Lead qualification | Lead scoring | MQL / SQL labels |
|---|---|---|---|
| What it is | A decision: pursue, park or drop | A rank: who to contact first | A hand-off stage between marketing and sales |
| Input | Fit, timing and a conversation | Points for fit and recent behaviour | Whichever rule the two teams agreed |
| Output | Yes, not now, or no | A number per lead | A tag in the CRM |
| When | Before and during the first conversation | Continuously, before outreach | When a lead crosses a threshold |
| Who owns it | Sales | Marketing and sales together | Marketing (MQL), sales (SQL) |

Scoring narrows the list, qualification settles each name on it, and the MQL and SQL labels record where a lead sits between the two teams. A one-person company needs the first two and can ignore the third. The lesson on [what lead scoring is](/learn/what-is-lead-scoring) gives a ten-row model that does the ranking.

## When should you disqualify a lead?

As soon as one of the three checks fails and you can see it will not change within a quarter. Most pages treat disqualification as a loss; it is the saving. RevenueHero's analysis of its own inbound data found that 39% of companies run no disqualification at all, and that companies which do disqualify convert a higher share of the leads they keep into meetings ([RevenueHero, Lead qualification 101](https://www.revenuehero.io/blog/what-is-lead-qualification)). The dataset is one vendor's customers, so read it as a signal rather than a census.

Disqualifying does not mean ignoring. A timing fail gets a dated note and a reason, and the person hears from you when the reason changes. A fit fail gets a short, honest message.

```message
Thanks for the reply. From what you've described, we're not the right fit for a team your size, and I'd rather say so now than waste your time on a call. If that changes, I'm easy to find.
```

Qualification by signal is how we launched. We ran our own platform on LinkedIn, targeted founders who were posting about cold outreach burnout, let the conversational AI handle replies, and the system booked 14 qualified demos in 14 days with zero manual outreach, at a reply rate 4x our previous cold campaigns. Most of those leads came from intent signals caught in real time. Checks one and two were done before the first message, which is why the demos that got booked were qualified ones.

## How to use lead qualification this week

1. **Write your three pass conditions from your last ten customers.** Company size, the title you sold to, and the signal that preceded the deal. Those three lines are your card's first rows, and anything far outside them is your automatic fail.
2. **Qualify twenty names before you message anyone.** Take this week's engagers, commenters and question-askers in your category, run the card on each, and message only the ones that pass fit and timing. The guide on [how to qualify leads before outreach](/guides/qualify-leads-before-outreach) walks through the five-minute version.
3. **Log every disqualification with a reason and a date.** After a month, read the reasons. If "no budget" is the most common one, you are asking BANT's question; switch to means.

## FAQ

### What does it mean to qualify a lead?

To qualify a lead is to check, before spending real time on it, that the company can buy from you, that the problem you solve is live for them now, and that the person you are talking to owns that problem or signs for the fix. A lead that passes is worked this week; one that fails is dropped or parked with a date to look again.

### What is a qualified lead?

A qualified lead is a person at a company that fits your ideal customer profile, has shown a recent sign of needing what you sell, and is in a position to act on it. Teams add their own labels: a marketing qualified lead has crossed a marketing threshold such as a download, and a sales qualified lead has been checked by a salesperson and accepted as a real opportunity.

### What is the difference between lead qualification and lead scoring?

Lead scoring ranks leads with points for fit and recent behaviour so you know who to contact first. Lead qualification is the decision, made before and during the first conversation, about whether a specific lead can become a deal. Scoring sorts the list; qualification says yes, not now or no to each name. Small teams often run qualification alone, using a short checklist instead of a model.

### Which lead qualification framework should I use?

For a company of one to 200 people with deals that close inside a quarter, the three questions on this page plus FAINT's view of money are enough: can they buy, is the problem live, is this the owner. BANT works when buyers arrive with a set budget. MEDDICC and GPCTBA earn their length in enterprise deals with many stakeholders and a formal process.

### Who is responsible for qualifying leads?

Sales owns the decision, because sales pays for a wrong one with its time. Marketing usually owns the first filter, such as the fit rules that decide what becomes a marketing qualified lead. In a company of one to ten people the same person does both, which is why a written card matters: it keeps the decision consistent when you are tired or the pipeline is thin.

### When should you disqualify a lead?

The moment one of the three checks fails and nothing within a quarter will change it: a company far outside your size range, no sign the problem exists, or a contact with no path to the owner. Fit fails are dropped with a short, honest message. Timing fails go on a dated watch list with the reason, so the lead comes back when the reason changes.
