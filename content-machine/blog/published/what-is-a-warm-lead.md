**A warm lead is a person who fits your customer profile and has recently done something that gives your first message a specific reason to exist.** They opened your pricing page twice, a customer introduced them, or they asked in public which tool to buy. The short answer to "what is a warm lead" is a lead you can write to without pretending: the first line names what they did, and they recognise it.

## Why does a warm lead matter?

Because the alternative is writing to people who have given you nothing to say. A cold message opens with your company; a warm message opens with the reader's own action, and the reader can tell the difference in the first line.

It also matters because buyers now do most of the work out of your sight. In a Gartner survey of 646 B2B buyers run from August to September 2025, 67% said they prefer a rep-free experience ([Gartner, 9 March 2026](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2026-03-09-gartner-sales-survey-finds-67-percent-of-b2b-buyers-prefer-a-rep-free-experience)). A second release from the same survey says buyers used an average of seven information sources during a recent purchase, and that 69% still prefer to validate what they learned with a sales rep ([Gartner, 20 May 2026](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2026-05-20-gartner-survey-finds-sixty-nine-percent-of-b-two-b-buyers-turn-to-sales-reps-to-validate-ai-generated-insights)). The research happens without you; the moments where it shows are the warm leads.

A labelled example. Suppose a three-person agency sells HubSpot setups. On Monday a marketing manager at a 40-person SaaS downloads their migration checklist. On Tuesday a different marketing manager posts on LinkedIn asking which CRM her team should move to. Neither has asked for a call. Both are warm leads, for different reasons, and both can receive a message whose first sentence is about them. The other 400 names on the agency's list cannot.

## Where does a warm lead come from?

Most explainers define a warm lead as someone who has engaged with your company. That covers one source of warmth out of three, and for a company with no inbound traffic yet it is the one source they do not have.

**They came to you.** Pricing page visits, a repeat read of your case studies, a trial sign-up that stalled, a reply to your newsletter. This is the classic warm lead, and the one every CRM scores. The weakness is that it only exists once people have already found you.

**Someone brought them.** A referral from a customer, an introduction from a partner, a former champion who moved to a new company and remembers you. These leads carry borrowed trust. The warmth belongs to the relationship, so the first message should name the person who made the introduction, not your product.

**They acted in public about the problem.** A founder asks in a LinkedIn post which outreach tool to switch to. A head of sales comments under a competitor's announcement that the pricing change is "the last straw". A marketing lead follows three vendors in your category in one week. None of these people has heard of you. They are still warm, because they have told the world they are deciding, and your first line can quote them. For a company with no pipeline, this is the only kind of warm lead available on day one, and the lesson on [what a buying signal is](/learn/what-is-a-buying-signal) covers how to tell a real one from noise.

The first source is about their relationship with you. The third is about their timing. A newsletter subscriber of three years and a founder asking for recommendations this morning both count as warm on most lists, and only one of them is deciding now. When you have to choose whom to write to first, timing beats familiarity.

## Warm lead vs cold lead vs hot lead

The three temperatures describe how much the lead has done, and how urgently. The useful question for each is what you can honestly put in the first line of a message.

| | Cold lead | Warm lead | Hot lead |
|---|---|---|---|
| What they have done | Nothing you can point to; they match your profile | One recent, specific action: with you, through a referral, or in public about the problem | Asked for the sale or close to it: demo request, pricing question, "send me a proposal" |
| What they know about you | Usually nothing | Something, or nothing yet if the action was public | Enough to ask |
| First line you can write | Your assumption about their problem | The thing they did or said, quoted | Confirmation of the next step they asked for |
| Timing | Unknown | Recent, usually within a few weeks | Now |
| Right move | Research or wait for a signal | One message that references the action, then a question | Answer the same day, propose a date |
| Who owns it | Marketing, or nobody | Whoever can write the message today | Sales |

Two neighbouring terms cause confusion. A **marketing qualified lead (MQL)** is a warm lead that has crossed a score threshold your marketing team set, usually from first-source behaviour such as downloads and page visits. A **sales qualified lead (SQL)** is a lead that sales has checked for fit, need and timing and accepted. Both are labels for stages inside a company's process; "warm" describes the lead's behaviour regardless of your process. A referral with no score is warm. An MQL who downloaded one ebook for a university assignment is not. If you have no scoring model, the lesson on [what lead scoring is](/learn/what-is-lead-scoring) shows a small one you can copy.

A note on the numbers you will see elsewhere. The articles ranking for this term quote conversion rates of 1 to 3% for cold leads, 5 to 15% for warm and 20 to 40% for hot. [ZoomInfo's page](https://pipeline.zoominfo.com/sales/how-to-differentiate-between-warm-and-hot-leads) attributes them to "industry benchmarks" without naming one, and [monday.com's page](https://monday.com/blog/crm-and-sales/what-are-warm-leads/) gives the same ranges with no source at all. Treat them as a rough direction, not a benchmark. Your own numbers, from twenty leads of each temperature, will tell you more than any of these pages can.

## The 60-second warmth test

Before you call a lead warm, answer three questions. Copy the table into your notes and run every new name through it. A lead is warm when all three answers are yes.

| Question | Yes looks like | No looks like |
|---|---|---|
| Can I name one specific thing this person did, in one sentence, without the word "might"? | "You asked on Thursday which CRM to move to" | "Marketing managers are often looking at CRMs" |
| Did it happen recently enough to still be true? | This week, or this month for a new role or a funding round | A webinar they attended last year |
| Do they fit the customer profile well enough that I would want the deal? | Right role, right company size, a problem you solve | A student, a competitor's employee, a company ten times too large |

One yes short, and you have context or a cold lead, not a warm one. The third question is the one people skip. A perfectly phrased public question from a company you would never sell to is still a no, and the lesson on [what an ideal customer profile is](/learn/what-is-an-ideal-customer-profile) gives the five parts of a profile to check it against, including the disqualifiers.

## How to use warm leads this week

Three actions, in order, each under an hour the first time.

1. **Sort what you already have.** Open your inbox, your CRM if you have one, and your LinkedIn notifications from the last 30 days. Run every name through the three questions. Most lists shrink to a handful of real warm leads and a long tail of names that were only ever familiar. Write to the handful first, this week, and let the tail wait.

2. **Add the third source.** Pick one competitor's LinkedIn page and two phrases your buyers use when they are deciding (for a CRM agency: "which CRM" and "switching from HubSpot"). Each morning, read who commented and who posted, run them through the test, and note the ones who pass. By Friday you will have warm leads who have never heard of you. When the daily reading becomes the job, Intentsly's [LinkedIn signals](/features/linkedin-signals) watch keyword posts, post engagement and competitor followers for you and score each person against your customer profile, so the morning list is ready before you open LinkedIn.

3. **Write to the action, not to the title.** The first sentence of the message names the thing they did; the second asks one question about it; there is no third paragraph. The guide on [how to write a LinkedIn outreach message](/guides/write-linkedin-outreach-message) has the structure and the examples. We ran this on ourselves at launch: our own LinkedIn signals, aimed at founders who were complaining in public about cold-outreach burnout, with the conversation handled by our AI SDR. The result was 14 qualified demos in 14 days with zero manual outreach, and a reply rate 4x our previous cold campaigns. Most of those leads came from signals caught in real time, which means they were warm in the third sense before we sent a word.

## FAQ

### What is the difference between a warm lead and a hot lead?

A warm lead has done something specific and recent that gives you a reason to write, but has not asked to buy: a pricing visit, a referral, a public question about your category. A hot lead has asked for the sale or something close to it, such as a demo, a quote or a proposal. Warm leads get one message that quotes the action; hot leads get an answer the same day and a proposed date.

### Is a referral a warm lead?

Yes, and usually a strong one. A referral carries the trust of the person who made it, so the lead will read your message because of that name, not because of your product. Open with the introducer, say in one line why they thought of you, and ask one question. A referral still needs the fit check: a kind introduction to the wrong company is a warm lead you should decline politely.

### What is the difference between a warm lead and an MQL?

An MQL is a label your marketing process gives a lead once it crosses a score threshold, usually built from downloads, page visits and email clicks. "Warm" describes behaviour regardless of process: a referral or a public buying question is warm with no score at all. Most MQLs are warm leads; many warm leads never become MQLs because they never touched your website.

### How long does a lead stay warm?

As long as the action that made them warm is still true. A public question cools within days, because other people answer it. A pricing visit is worth acting on within a day or two. A new role or a funding round stays relevant for several weeks, because the need builds over the first quarter. Re-run the three-question test on anything older than a month before you treat it as warm.

### How do you warm up a cold lead?

You cannot warm a lead by sending more messages; warmth comes from something they do. Two things help. Give them a reason to act, such as a useful answer in a thread they are reading or a short piece they would share. And watch for the moment they act in public about the problem, which turns a cold lead into a warm one without any nurture sequence at all.
