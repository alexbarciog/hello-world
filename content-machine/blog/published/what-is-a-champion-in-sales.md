**A champion in sales is a person inside the buyer's company who wants your deal to happen, has enough influence to move it, and sells it to colleagues when you are not in the room.** That is the short answer to "what is a champion in sales": a champion is a role, not a job title. A junior analyst can be one; a vice president who likes you but does nothing is not. The same words sometimes mean a top-performing rep, which is covered below.

## Why does a champion matter in a deal?

A champion matters because most B2B purchases are decided in meetings you are not invited to. Someone in those meetings has to make your case, answer the objection you never heard, and keep the decision on the agenda. If nobody does, the deal quietly loses to "let's revisit next quarter" rather than to a competitor.

The numbers point the same way. Gong's analysis of 1.8 million deals closed in 2024 found that 77% of deals involved more than one buyer contact, that deals which closed had twice as many buyer contacts as deals which did not, and that in deals over $50K, working several contacts at once lifted win rates by an average of 130% ([Gong, 2025, updated May 2026](https://www.gong.io/blog/data-shows-top-reps-dont-just-sell-they-orchestrate-with-ai)). A champion is the contact who opens the door to the others.

Take a hypothetical example. Halden Logistics, a 60-person freight broker, is evaluating outbound tools. Your contact, Priya, runs sales operations and has no budget authority. But she wrote the internal memo on why reply rates fell, she reports to the VP who owns the budget, and she will be blamed if the new tool fails. She has a reason to care, a way to influence the decision and a willingness to argue for it. The VP is the decision maker. Priya is the champion. You usually meet the champion first.

## What does a champion in sales actually do?

A champion does four jobs you cannot do yourself. The qualification methods that use the term, MEDDIC and its longer cousin MEDDPICC, where a C stands for Champion, describe the role as someone who "holds real influence and power, has a stake in the outcome" and advocates for the deal internally ([MEDDICC](https://meddicc.com/resources/no-champion-no-deal-your-quarterback-in-the-sales-process)). Force Management reduces it to three characteristics: power and influence, actively sells on your behalf, and a vested interest in the outcome ([Force Management](https://www.forcemanagement.com/seller-blog/coaches-vs-champions-know-the-difference)). In practice those traits turn into the following work.

1. **They tell you how the decision really gets made.** Who signs, who can veto, what the last vendor got wrong, which budget line this comes from. Anthony Iannarino lists this as "understanding the client's company", one of the [five things you need from a champion](https://thesalesblog.com/blog/the-five-things-you-need-from-your-sales-champion).
2. **They get you in front of the person with the budget.** Not "I'll mention it to her", but a calendar invite with the economic buyer on it.
3. **They sell when you are absent.** They forward your one-pager, answer the CFO's question on Tuesday, and report back what was said.
4. **They carry risk for you.** They put their own name on the recommendation. That is why a champion needs a personal win: a problem that goes away, a project that succeeds, a promotion that gets closer.

A contact who does the first job but none of the others is useful. They are not a champion.

## Champion vs coach vs decision maker: who can do what?

The word champion gets mixed up with five neighbouring roles, and the mix-up is expensive, because each role can do a different thing for you. Here is one table for all of them. An **economic buyer** is the person who controls the budget and can say yes without asking anyone. A **coach** gives you information and goodwill but has no power to move the decision. A **sponsor** is a senior person who has agreed the project should happen but is not working the deal day to day. A **mobilizer** is Challenger's term for a stakeholder who can build consensus for change inside their company, whether or not they favour you.

| Role | Wants your deal? | Can influence the decision? | Can sign? | What they can do for you | What they cannot do |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Champion | Yes, for their own reasons | Yes | Usually not | Sell internally, get you access, warn you early | Approve the budget alone |
| Coach | Friendly, not committed | No | No | Explain the org chart, share gossip, review your deck | Move the deal a single step |
| Economic buyer | Maybe | Yes | Yes | Say yes | Spend time learning your product |
| Decision maker | Maybe | Yes, on their part of it | Sometimes | Decide their piece: technical fit, security, legal | Override the economic buyer |
| Sponsor | Yes, in principle | Yes, when asked | Often | Unblock a stalled step, lend authority | Chase the details every week |
| Mobilizer | Not necessarily you | Yes | Varies | Build consensus for change | Guarantee you win that change |

Two things follow. First, the champion and the economic buyer are rarely the same person in a company of 500 people, and usually the same person in a company of 15. When we ran our own LinkedIn signals and AI SDR for two weeks and booked 14 qualified demos, the people who replied were founders complaining about cold outreach burnout, so the person who felt the pain was also the person who could say yes. If you sell to small companies, your champion search is mostly a founder search. The lesson on [what an ideal customer profile is](/learn/what-is-an-ideal-customer-profile) covers how to define that fit.

Second, a coach can become a champion, but only if they gain a stake or you find them one. The most common error in a stalled deal is logging a coach as the champion because they answer emails quickly.

## How do you test whether someone is a real champion?

Ask them to do something a coach would not. Enthusiasm costs the contact nothing, while access and effort cost them time and standing. Four tests, each a specific ask, borrowed from the MEDDICC and Force Management material above and from the [a79 four-test framework](https://www.a79.ai/resources/sales-champion-qualification-framework):

| Test | The ask | A champion | A coach |
|---|---|---|---|
| Access | "Can you get the budget owner on our next call?" | Sends the invite within days | "Let me see what I can do" with no date |
| Effort | "Could you review this one-page business case and tell me what your CFO will push back on?" | Returns it with edits and the objections | Says it looks great |
| Risk | "What would make your team say no to this?" | Names the sceptic and the reason | Says there are no real blockers |
| Language | "How would you explain why now to your boss?" | Gives a sentence in their company's words | Repeats your pitch back to you |

Fail two of the four and you have a coach, which tells you exactly what to work on. Go back to the first test and make the ask smaller: an introduction to one more person, not the CFO. The [guide to qualifying leads before outreach](/guides/qualify-leads-before-outreach) covers the fit checks you run before any of this.

## How do you find a champion before the first call?

Look for the person who is already arguing for change in public. A champion has a personal stake and a willingness to speak up, and both leave traces on LinkedIn long before they take a meeting. Challenger's research on 700 customer stakeholders, scored on 135 attributes, sorted them into Mobilizers, who move their organisation toward a decision, Talkers, who share information but cannot drive change, and Blockers, and found that sellers who target Mobilizers are 31% more likely to be high performers ([Challenger](https://challengerinc.com/the-challenger-customer-profiles/)). The Mobilizer and the champion are not identical, but the first place to look for one is the other.

Three public behaviours mark a would-be champion:

1. **They post or comment about the problem you solve**, in their own words, with a view. Search the LinkedIn Posts tab for `"reply rates" "outbound" "anyone else"` or `"we switched from" <tool you replace>` and sort by Latest. The person complaining is the one with the stake.
2. **They engage with your competitors' and your category's posts**: likes are weak, comments with an opinion are strong. Someone who comments "we tried this and here is what happened" under a competitor's launch is already doing the champion's job for somebody.
3. **They have owned a change before.** A profile that says "led the move from X to Y" or "built the outbound function" belongs to someone who is trusted with decisions and knows how to get one through.

Doing this by hand means a daily search and a spreadsheet. Intentsly watches LinkedIn keyword posts and the people engaging with competitor and industry posts, then scores each person against your ideal customer profile, so the "who already cares about this" list arrives ranked instead of being rebuilt every morning; [LinkedIn signals](/features/linkedin-signals) are the only source it monitors, so job boards and funding news stay a manual check. The lesson on [what a buying signal is](/learn/what-is-a-buying-signal) explains why a comment with an opinion counts for more than a like.

A would-be champion found this way is still a stranger, so the first message quotes what they said and asks one question about it. The [guide to finding decision makers on LinkedIn](/guides/find-decision-makers-on-linkedin) covers the next step, mapping from that person to the budget owner.

## Is a "sales champion" also a top-performing rep?

Yes, the same two words carry a second meaning. Some pages, such as [Breakcold's glossary entry](https://www.breakcold.com/explain/sales-champion), use "sales champion" for a salesperson who consistently beats target. In deal qualification, in MEDDIC and on this page, a champion is on the buyer's side. In a CRM field or a forecast review, the buyer-side meaning is almost always the one intended; in a sales-training brochure, check.

## How to use this this week

Three actions, each under an hour.

1. **Audit your open deals for coaches wearing champion badges.** For every opportunity past the first call, write the contact's name and answer the four tests honestly. Any deal where the answer is "coach" gets one task: a specific ask that would turn them into a champion or prove they cannot be one.
2. **Find one would-be champion in a target account before you contact anyone there.** Run the two search strings above for a company on your list. Read what the person wrote. Open with that.
3. **Give your current champion something to forward.** One page: the problem in their words, the cost of waiting, what changes in 30 days, and the one question the CFO will ask with its answer. Champions sell when you are absent, and they sell better with a prop. The free [Buying Signal Playbook](/signal-playbook) includes a script per signal you can adapt for that page.

## FAQ

### What is a champion in sales in simple terms?

A champion is a person at the company you are selling to who wants your deal to succeed, has enough influence to affect the decision, and argues for it when you are not there. They are usually not the person who signs the contract. They are the person who gets you in front of that person and keeps the deal moving between your meetings.

### What is the difference between a champion and a coach in sales?

A coach gives you information and goodwill: who reports to whom, what happened with the last vendor, how your deck landed. A champion does that and also acts: they introduce you to the budget owner, argue for you in internal meetings and put their own credibility behind the recommendation. The test is simple. Ask for an introduction with a date. A champion delivers it; a coach explains why it is hard.

### Is a champion the same as a decision maker?

No. A decision maker controls part or all of the decision; the one who controls the budget is often called the economic buyer. A champion influences the decision and may have no authority to make it. In small companies the two are often the same founder. In larger companies the champion is typically a manager or specialist who feels the problem daily and reports to the person who can approve the spend.

### What is a champion in MEDDIC?

In MEDDIC, and in MEDDPICC, one C stands for Champion: a person inside the account with power and influence, a personal stake in the outcome, and a willingness to sell on your behalf. The method treats a missing or unproven champion as a qualification gap, which is why its users repeat the phrase "no champion, no deal". It is a check on the deal, not a compliment to the contact.

### How do you find a champion for a deal?

Look for the person who is already pushing for change. Inside an account, that is whoever owns the problem, has led a change before, and will be judged on the result. Before you have an account, LinkedIn shows the same traits in public: posts and comments about the problem in their own words, opinions left under competitor and industry posts, and a profile that mentions leading a switch or building a function.

### What happens when your champion leaves the company?

The deal usually stalls, because the person carrying it internally is gone and nobody else has the same stake. Two defences work. Build a second relationship early, ideally with the economic buyer, so the deal is not held by one thread. And follow the champion: someone who argued for your product once will often want it again at their new company, so a job-change post is a reason to reach out.
