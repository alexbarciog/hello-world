**First-party intent data is buyer behaviour that you record yourself, on properties you own, under your own privacy notice: pricing-page visits, trial sign-ups, demo requests, email clicks, replies to your messages.** The short answer to "what is first-party intent data" is evidence of interest that nobody sold you. It is the most accurate intent data a company can hold, and the most limited, because it only ever describes people who have already found you.

## Why does first-party intent data matter?

It matters because it is the one kind of intent data you can trust without checking. A bought topic score tells you a company is "surging" on a subject. Your own data tells you that a named person opened your pricing page twice on Tuesday and started a trial on Wednesday.

A labelled example. Suppose a six-person agency sells HubSpot migrations. Last week 300 people visited the site: forty read the migration pricing page, nine downloaded the migration checklist, and two filled in the contact form. Those two are the hottest leads the agency will get all week, and the nine downloaders are next. Nobody sold the agency a list; it read its own analytics.

The same example shows the limit. The agency has 2,000 potential customers in its market. First-party intent data says nothing about the 1,700 who did not visit, including the ones switching CRMs this quarter who have never heard of it.

## What counts as first-party intent data?

One question settles it: **did the behaviour happen on something you own, and would your privacy notice cover it?** If yes, it is first-party. If the behaviour happened somewhere else and someone sold or shared it with you, it is not.

The table lists the usual sources from weakest to strongest, with what each one proves. The "proves" column is the point; most explainers list the sources and stop.

| Source | What you see | What it proves | What it does not prove |
|---|---|---|---|
| Blog and content views | Page, time on page, return visits | Interest in the topic | Interest in buying anything |
| Pricing and comparison pages | Which plan or rival was viewed | The visitor is weighing options | That they have budget or authority |
| Gated downloads | Name, email, the asset chosen | A problem they are willing to trade an email for | A timeline |
| Email engagement | Opens, clicks, replies | Attention to your message | Opens mean little; replies mean a lot |
| Webinar or event attendance | Registration, attendance, questions asked | Time invested in your topic | Whether they are the buyer or the researcher |
| Trial or product usage | Sign-up, features used, invites sent | Active evaluation | That the trial is for a purchase, not a comparison |
| Demo and contact requests | The ask, in their words | Readiness to talk | Nothing missing: this is the strongest signal you own |
| Sales conversations | Pain, timeline, budget, decision makers | Everything above, stated directly | Only exists after the first call |

Two neighbouring terms get confused with it. **Zero-party data** is information a person gives you on purpose, such as an answer to "What are you trying to fix?" on a form; for a small team it is simply the best kind of first-party data. **Second-party intent data** is another company's first-party data shared or sold to you, for example a review site reporting which companies compared your product with a rival. It is accurate about the behaviour, but it was collected under someone else's terms.

## First-party intent data vs third-party intent data

Third-party intent data is behaviour recorded on sites you do not own, aggregated by a provider and licensed to you, usually as a topic score per company. The useful comparison is what each one can and cannot tell you.

| | First-party intent data | Third-party intent data |
|---|---|---|
| Where it is recorded | Your site, product, email, events | Publisher networks, review sites, ad bidstreams |
| Who it identifies | Usually a named person or a known account | Usually an account, rarely a person |
| What it covers | People who already found you | The wider market, including strangers |
| How current it is | Real time, in your own tools | Delivered on the provider's schedule |
| Consent | Your privacy notice, your lawful basis | The provider's consent chain, which you cannot audit |
| Cost | Free or already paid for | A contract, priced for larger teams |
| Can you check it? | Yes, open the log | No, you get a score |

The coverage gap is larger than most explainers admit. In the 6sense 2025 Buyer Experience Report, a survey of more than 4,000 B2B buyers, the balance of research time to seller engagement was 60/40, 94% of buying groups had ranked their preferred vendors before first contact with any seller, and buyers bought from their preliminary favourite 77% of the time ([6sense, Business Wire, 12 November 2025](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20251112018032/en/)). Most of that ranking happens on pages and in conversations you cannot see.

Gartner's survey of 646 B2B buyers, run between August and September 2025, points the same way: 67% said they prefer a rep-free buying experience and 45% used AI during a recent purchase ([Gartner press release, 9 March 2026](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2026-03-09-gartner-sales-survey-finds-67-percent-of-b2b-buyers-prefer-a-rep-free-experience)). If a buyer prefers not to talk to you, your first-party data about them will consist of a few anonymous page views until the day they are ready, and by then their shortlist is written.

The two-way comparison leaves out the category a small team can actually use. **Public signals** are things a person says or does in the open: a LinkedIn comment under a competitor's post, a "which tool should I use" question, a new follow of a rival's company page. You do not own the platform, so they are not first-party, and nobody aggregated or scored them, so they are not third-party either. They name the person, they are free to read, and you can verify each one yourself. The [buying signal](/learn/what-is-a-buying-signal) lesson covers how to judge one.

## Is a LinkedIn comment first-party intent data?

Apply the ownership test. A comment under your own company post happened on a platform you do not own, but it is a direct, public interaction with you, and most teams file it as first-party engagement in the CRM. A comment under a competitor's post is a public signal, not first-party data. It is often the stronger buying sign of the two, because the person is comparing options in the open rather than reading your blog.

The honest rule for a new or small company: you will have almost no first-party intent data in the first year, because it is a function of traffic you have not earned yet. When we launched Intentsly, our own pipeline could not come from our own site. We ran our LinkedIn signals and AI SDR on founders complaining about cold-outreach burnout and booked 14 qualified demos in 14 days with zero manual outreach, at a reply rate 4x our previous cold campaigns. Those leads came from public signals caught in real time, not from first-party data we did not yet have.

## How to use first-party intent data this week

Three actions, each under an hour.

1. **Rank your own sources.** Copy the first table, delete the rows you do not have, and write the one action you will take on each remaining row. A demo request gets a reply within the hour. A pricing-page visit by a known contact gets one short message that mentions the page. A blog view gets nothing. Writing this down stops the mistake of treating every page view as a lead.

2. **Fix the consent line before the tooling.** Check that your privacy notice names the behaviour you track and states the lawful basis you rely on. Under GDPR, Article 6 sets out those bases, including consent in point (a) and legitimate interests in point (f) ([Art. 6 GDPR, Lawfulness of processing](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-6-gdpr/)). This is not legal advice; ask your adviser which basis fits your tracking.

3. **Add one public source for the people you cannot see.** Pick one competitor's LinkedIn page and read its new commenters each morning for a week, noting who matches your ideal customer. By Friday you will have a short list your analytics could never have produced. If the daily reading is the part that does not get done, Intentsly's [LinkedIn signals](/features/linkedin-signals) watch those posts, keywords and competitor followers continuously and score each person against your customer profile, so the list is ready before you are.

The decision rule in one sentence: use first-party intent data to decide who to call first among the people who already found you, and use public signals to find the ones who have not.

## FAQ

### What is first-party intent data in simple terms?

It is the record of what people do on things you own: your website, your product, your emails, your events. A pricing-page visit, a trial sign-up or a demo request is first-party intent data because you recorded it yourself, under your own privacy notice. Nobody collected it elsewhere and sold it to you, which is what makes it accurate and also what limits it.

### What is the difference between first-party and third-party intent data?

First-party intent data is behaviour on your own properties, usually tied to a named person, available in real time and covered by your own consent. Third-party intent data is behaviour collected on other sites by a provider and licensed to you, usually as a topic score per company, delivered on a schedule and collected under terms you cannot inspect. The first is precise but narrow; the second is broad but blurry.

### What are examples of first-party intent data?

Common examples are visits to pricing or comparison pages, gated content downloads, email replies, webinar attendance, free-trial activity, demo or contact requests, and what prospects say on sales calls. The strongest examples are the ones that cost the person effort, such as a demo request or a trial with real usage. A single blog view is also first-party, but it proves almost nothing on its own.

### Is first-party intent data GDPR compliant?

It can be, and it is the easiest kind to make compliant, because you control the collection. Compliance depends on your privacy notice naming the tracking and on a lawful basis under Article 6 of the GDPR, such as consent or legitimate interests. Bought data depends on a provider's consent chain you cannot audit. Confirm your own basis with a legal adviser rather than assuming either kind is covered.

### Is first-party intent data enough on its own?

For a company with steady traffic and a known buyer, it is enough to decide who to contact first among people already on the site. It is not enough to find buyers who have never visited, which for most small companies is nearly all of them. Pair it with public signals, such as what prospects say and do on LinkedIn, to cover the part of the market your analytics cannot see.
