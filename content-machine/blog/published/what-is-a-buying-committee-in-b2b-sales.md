**A buying committee in B2B sales is the group of people inside a company who jointly decide whether to buy, each judging the purchase from their own job.** That is the short answer to "what is a buying committee in B2B sales": not one decision maker but several, usually from more than one department, who have to agree, or at least stop objecting, before a contract is signed. The roles, the real size, and how to spot the members before the first call follow.

## Why does the buying committee matter to a seller?

It matters because the person you are talking to is almost never the whole decision. Forrester's survey of nearly 18,000 business buyers found that, on average, 13 internal stakeholders and nine external participants influence a buying decision, and that the number rises for more expensive or complex purchases ([Forrester, January 2026](https://www.forrester.com/blogs/state-of-business-buying-2026)). The earlier edition of the same survey, with more than 16,000 buyers, found that 91% of purchases stall at some point ([Forrester, 2024](https://www.forrester.com/blogs/state-of-business-buying-2024/)). Most of those stalls happen in a meeting the seller is not in, when a committee member who was never contacted asks a question nobody prepared for.

Take a hypothetical example. Marrow Freight, a 90-person logistics broker, is looking at outbound tools. Dana, the head of sales, loves the demo and says she will "run it past the team". The team is the committee: an operations manager who will have to make the data flow into HubSpot, a finance lead who signs anything over a few thousand a year, and the founder, who dislikes anything that touches the company's LinkedIn accounts. Dana is one voice of four. If the seller only ever speaks to Dana, three unanswered objections are waiting in the room.

The term itself comes from research on industrial buying. Thomas Bonoma's 1982 Harvard Business Review article "Major Sales: Who Really Does the Buying?" argued that power in a purchase does not correlate neatly with organisational rank, and that sellers who never find the people who actually decide lose sales without knowing why ([HBR Store](https://store.hbr.org/product/major-sales-who-really-does-the-buying-hbr-classic/R0607P)).

## Who sits on a buying committee in B2B sales?

The same handful of roles appear in almost every committee, whatever the titles. The classic role list, as taught in business schools, is initiator, gatekeeper, user, technical influencer, financial influencer, decider, champion and coach ([Baylor Keller Center](https://kellercenter.hankamer.baylor.edu/news/story/2016/identifying-and-managing-buying-center)). What matters to a seller is less the label than the question each person is quietly asking and the sentence they say when they are about to stop the deal.

| Role | Typical title at a small or mid-size company | The question they are asking | What they say before they kill the deal |
|---|---|---|---|
| Initiator | The person who felt the problem first: a rep, a marketer, an ops lead | "Does this fix the thing that is hurting me?" | "I'll find another way around it." |
| Champion | A manager who owns the problem and reports to the budget holder | "Will this make me look right for pushing it?" | "I can't get it on the agenda this quarter." |
| User | Whoever will log in every day | "Will this add work to my week?" | "It's fine, but we're busy right now." |
| Technical evaluator | IT lead, RevOps, or the engineer who owns the CRM | "What breaks, and what data leaves the building?" | "We need to review the security side first." |
| Economic buyer | Finance lead, VP or founder with budget authority | "What is the return, and what is the risk of saying yes?" | "Let's revisit next quarter." |
| Decider | The one person who can say yes alone, often the economic buyer | "Is this the right call for the company?" | Silence. |
| Gatekeeper | Executive assistant, procurement, legal | "Should this even reach the decider?" | "Send the details over and we'll be in touch." |

Several roles can live in one person, and one role can be split across several. In a company of 15 people, the initiator, champion, economic buyer and decider are often the same founder. In a company of 1,500, the technical evaluator alone may be a three-person security review. The lesson on [what a champion in sales is](/learn/what-is-a-champion-in-sales) covers the one role you need on your side before any of the others will talk to you.

## How big is a B2B buying committee really?

Between about four and thirteen people, depending on who is counting and what they count. The figures that circulate are not contradictory once you know what each measured.

| Figure | What it counts | Sample | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| 13 internal stakeholders plus 9 external participants | Everyone who influences the decision, including consultants and peers outside the company | Nearly 18,000 global business buyers | [Forrester, 2026](https://www.forrester.com/blogs/state-of-business-buying-2026) |
| 6 to 7 members on average; about 15 for IT services deals over $5 million | The buying team that actually works the purchase, in technology buying | Gartner research | [Gartner](https://gcom.pdo.aws.gartner.com/en/articles/help-your-tech-buying-team-work-more-effectively) |
| 4.6 titles for companies under 200 people, 5.6 for 200 to 1,000, 6.0 above 1,000 | The decision-maker titles sellers say they target per deal | 1,871 ideal-customer-profile submissions, January 2024 to February 2026 | [Belkins](https://belkins.io/blog/b2b-buying-committee-study) |

Forrester counts influence, so friends, advisors and peer reviewers make the list. Gartner counts the working team. Belkins counts the titles a seller plans to reach, which is a floor, not a census. For a company of 1 to 200 people selling to similar companies, the Belkins figure is the most useful: plan for four or five people, and expect the founder or a technical lead to be among them. In that same study the CTO was named in 65.4% of deals aimed at companies under 200 people, and the CEO in 33.8% ([Belkins](https://belkins.io/blog/b2b-buying-committee-study)).

One more number describes what happens inside the committee. Gartner's 2026 Tech Buying Behavior Study found that 68% of buying teams experience unhealthy conflict, which comes with seven times more purchase regret and three times more project delays, and that when the executive sponsor moves on, unhealthy conflict rises by 75% ([Gartner, July 2026](https://gcomdr.pdo.aws.gartner.com/en/articles/tech-buying-behavior-in-the-age-of-ai)). A seller who helps the committee agree is doing work the committee is bad at on its own.

## Buying committee vs buying center vs buying group: what is the difference?

The three terms describe the same thing from three angles, and two neighbouring terms describe parts of it. A **buying center** is the academic name for the set of people involved in an organisational purchase, from the 1970s and 1980s industrial-marketing literature. A **buying group** is the same idea in the vocabulary of account-based marketing, where the unit of measurement is the group rather than the lead. A **buying committee** is the sales-floor word, and it implies something slightly stronger: the people have to meet, or at least sign off, together.

| Term | Means | Who uses it | How it differs |
|---|---|---|---|
| Buying committee | The people who jointly approve a purchase | Sales teams, deal reviews | Implies a formal or semi-formal sign-off |
| Buying center | Everyone who takes part in or influences an organisational purchase | Textbooks, marketing courses | Includes informal influencers who never attend a meeting |
| Buying group | The set of people at one account to engage as a unit | Account-based marketing, intent-data tools | A targeting and measurement unit, not a decision structure |
| Decision maker | The person with authority to say yes | Everyone | One member of the committee, not the whole |
| Champion | The member who wants your deal and sells it internally | Deal qualification (MEDDIC and similar) | Your ally on the committee, usually without signing power |

The practical rule: when a contact says "the committee", ask who is on it by name and job, not by title. When a marketing tool says "buying group", it means the accounts' people it has detected, which is a list to verify, not a committee that has met.

## How do you find the committee before the first call?

Look at who is already talking about the problem in public. Committee members leave traces on LinkedIn long before a deal exists: the initiator posts about the pain, the technical evaluator comments on a competitor's integration announcement, the user asks a question in a thread, and the economic buyer follows the companies that sell the category. A seller who reads those traces walks into the first call knowing three names instead of one.

Two searches do most of the work by hand. In the LinkedIn Posts tab, search `"<problem you solve>" "anyone else"` sorted by Latest to find initiators at companies that fit your profile. Then open your top competitors' company pages and read who commented on their last ten posts with an opinion rather than a thumbs-up; those people are evaluating the category right now, and their job titles tell you which committee seat they hold. Intentsly watches those same LinkedIn keyword posts and competitor-post engagers for you and scores each person against your ideal customer profile, so the "who at this account already cares" list arrives ranked instead of being rebuilt every morning; it monitors [LinkedIn signals](/features/linkedin-signals) only, so hiring pages and funding news stay a manual check.

What you cannot see in public is the gatekeeper and, often, the economic buyer. For those, the [guide to finding decision makers on LinkedIn](/guides/find-decision-makers-on-linkedin) covers how to map from the person who posted to the person who signs.

When we ran our own LinkedIn signals and AI SDR for two weeks and booked 14 qualified demos, the people who replied were founders complaining about cold outreach burnout, and the founder was the whole committee: initiator, champion and economic buyer in one chair. If you sell to companies under about 50 people, expect the same, and treat the second person who joins a call as a sign the deal is real rather than as a complication.

## How to use this lesson this week

Three actions, none longer than an hour.

1. **Draw the committee for your three biggest open deals.** For each, write the seven roles from the table above and put a name next to each one. Any role with no name is a question for your champion: "Who will Finance want to hear from before this is approved?" A committee you cannot draw is a deal you cannot forecast.
2. **Add a second thread to any deal that has one.** Pick the role most likely to object, usually the technical evaluator or the economic buyer, and send them one message that answers their question from the table, not your pitch. The lesson on [what a buying signal is](/learn/what-is-a-buying-signal) explains which of their public actions tell you the moment is right.
3. **Write the committee into your ideal customer profile.** Next to the company criteria, list the two or three seats you expect to fill at a company that size and the title that usually holds each. The lesson on [what an ideal customer profile is](/learn/what-is-an-ideal-customer-profile) shows where that section goes. It changes who you look for on the first day, not just who you ask for on the last.

## FAQ

### What is a buying committee in simple terms?

A buying committee is the group of people at a company who together decide whether to buy something. Each person checks the purchase from their own job: the user asks whether it adds work, the technical lead asks what it connects to, finance asks what it costs and returns, and one person has the authority to say yes. Most B2B purchases involve several of them, not one.

### Who is usually on a B2B buying committee?

Seven seats cover most committees: the initiator who felt the problem, a champion who pushes for the purchase, the users, a technical evaluator, the economic buyer who controls the budget, the decider who can say yes alone, and a gatekeeper such as procurement or legal. In small companies several seats belong to one person, often the founder. In large companies one seat can be a whole team.

### How many people are in a typical buying committee?

It depends on what you count. Forrester, counting everyone who influences a decision, puts it at 13 people inside the company. Gartner, counting the working team in technology purchases, finds six to seven. Belkins, counting the titles sellers target, finds 4.6 at companies under 200 people and 6.0 above 1,000. For small and mid-size targets, plan for four or five.

### Is a buying committee the same as a buying center or buying group?

They describe the same people from different angles. Buying center is the textbook term and includes informal influencers. Buying group is the account-based-marketing term and treats the people as a targeting unit. Buying committee is the sales term and implies a sign-off that happens together. A decision maker and a champion are single members of the committee, not synonyms for it.

### How do you identify the members of a buying committee?

Ask your champion, by name and job rather than title: "Who else has to be comfortable with this before it is approved?" Then confirm in public. Members post about the problem, comment on competitors' announcements, and follow the companies in your category on LinkedIn. Any role you cannot fill with a name is a gap in the deal, and the question to ask on the next call.

### Can a deal close without the whole committee agreeing?

Yes, if the decider says yes and nobody with veto power says no. But a deal signed over a silent objector tends to stall at rollout, which is where buyer regret shows up. The safer target is consent rather than enthusiasm: every seat has had its question answered, even the ones that never joined a call.
