To track job changes on LinkedIn for sales, name the people whose move would matter, then watch them on four surfaces: LinkedIn's free Catch Up tab and job-change notification for your connections, the Sales Navigator alert for saved leads, the Job Changes spotlight search for strangers at target accounts, and a monthly Past company search for the people who moved quietly. Log each move in a sheet, score it on four questions, and message the mover inside a week. Each step below gives the exact clicks.

## What you need to track job changes on LinkedIn for sales

- **A named list.** Every surface in this guide watches people you chose in advance. Pull closed-won contacts, the people who pushed for you in deals you lost, and the decision makers at your target accounts. If "champion" means different things to different people on your team, the lesson on [what a champion in sales is](/learn/what-is-a-champion-in-sales) gives a test to apply before a name goes on the list.
- **A LinkedIn account connected to as many of those people as possible.** The free surfaces only see first-degree connections.
- **Sales Navigator, optional.** Steps 3, 4 and the first half of Step 5 use it. Steps 1, 2 and 6 and the Google query in Step 5 work on a free account. The guide on [setting up Sales Navigator alerts](/guides/set-up-linkedin-sales-navigator-alerts) covers the plan gate if you are deciding whether to pay.
- **A sheet** with the columns in Step 6, and about two hours for setup. After that, plan on twenty minutes a week and an hour a month.

A job change, in this guide, means a person on your list taking a new title at a new company, or a new title with wider ownership at the same company. It is one of the shortest-fuse trigger events a small team can watch, and the lesson on [what a trigger event in sales is](/learn/what-is-a-trigger-event-in-sales) places it next to the others. LinkedIn's own Sales Navigator page claims [decision makers are 62% more receptive to outreach](https://business.linkedin.com/sell/sales-navigator/how-to-use) once they have moved; the page gives no method for that figure, so treat it as the vendor's claim and let your own reply rates decide.

## Step 1: Build the watch list before you open any tool

The list comes first because every alert, filter and feed below is blind to people you have not named. Three cohorts, from your CRM into one sheet:

| Cohort | Where to find them | Why a move matters |
|---|---|---|
| Past champions and current users | Closed-won deals, renewal contacts, support tickets | They already know the product. A new company is a new budget with a warm introduction built in. |
| Lost-deal champions | Closed-lost deals where one person argued for you | The company said no, the person said yes. A new company resets the first half. |
| Decision makers at target accounts | Your account list, the titles that sign for what you sell | An arrival changes who reviews vendors there. A departure tells you your contact is gone. |

Keep the list to a size you can watch by hand: 100 to 300 names for one seller, more if the team shares one sheet. UserGems, which sells job-change tracking, puts the turnover at [20% of people changing jobs every year, then 2% to 4% a month](https://www.usergems.com/contact-tracking-3x) depending on industry; it gives no method, but if the order of magnitude is right, a 200-name list produces a move or two a week, which is exactly the volume one person can handle without software.

For each name record company, title, LinkedIn URL, cohort, and whether you are connected. The connection column decides whether Step 2 or Step 3 covers the person.

## Step 2: Turn on LinkedIn's free job-change notifications

For first-degree connections LinkedIn shows job changes for free, in two places.

1. **The Catch Up tab.** Open **My Network** and click **Catch up**. LinkedIn introduced the tab to surface a connection's new job, work anniversary or hiring post with a one-tap reaction or a suggested message, according to [TechCrunch's report on the launch](https://techcrunch.com/?p=2661419). Check it twice a week and skip the anniversaries.
2. **The job-changes notification.** Click **Me**, then **Settings & Privacy**, then **Notifications**, then **Network catch-up updates**, then **Job changes**, and switch on **In-app notifications** (push too, if you want it on your phone). [Guideflow's walkthrough](https://www.guideflow.com/tutorial/how-to-turn-on-in-app-job-changes-notifications-on-linkedin), updated March 2026, shows each screen; LinkedIn moves labels around, so look for the same words if a menu has shifted.

Then connect with the people on your list you are not yet connected to, a few a day, with a one-line note that says why ("we worked together on the Harbor rollout in 2024"). Without the connection neither surface can see them.

What this misses matters as much as what it shows. It only covers connections. It only fires when the person edits their profile. And LinkedIn decides which updates to surface: Salesmotion's guide, written to sell an alternative, still describes the free notifications fairly when it says they arrive [weeks late or not at all](https://salesmotion.io/blog/champion-tracking-job-changes). Treat Step 2 as the alarm and the sheet in Step 6 as the record.

## Step 3: Save the list as Sales Navigator leads so the alert fires

If you pay for Sales Navigator, its job-change alert is the most dependable surface in this guide, with one condition: it fires only for leads you have saved. LinkedIn's how-to page puts it plainly: save leads and "let Sales Navigator send you alerts when they make a move, like changing jobs" ([LinkedIn, how to use Sales Navigator](https://business.linkedin.com/sell/sales-navigator/how-to-use)).

1. Create two lead lists, `Watch: champions` and `Watch: target accounts`.
2. Search each name from Step 1, open the profile, click **Save** and pick the list. Where a search result page lets you select several people, save in bulk.
3. Put the alert on email. Click your profile photo, then **Settings**, then **Email Preferences**, then the **Alert Preferences** section, and choose a daily summary. Evaboot's alerts guide lists the labels you are looking for as [Lead Changed Jobs and Lead Changed Roles](https://evaboot.com/blog/linkedin-sales-navigator-alerts), one for a new company and one for a new role at the same company, and notes the feed can be filtered by type, date or list.
4. Each week open the alerts feed on the Sales Navigator homepage, filter to lead alerts, and copy every job or role change into the sheet.

Two limits to state before you trust it. CatchIntent's guide, from a vendor that sells a replacement, says the alerts can [lag the real update by one to four weeks](https://catchintent.com/blog/track-job-changes-linkedin-sales/); that is its observation rather than a measured figure, but it matches the general rule that LinkedIn learns about a move when the profile is edited, not when the person starts. And the saved list is the ceiling: an alert will never mention someone you did not save.

## Step 4: Run the Job Changes spotlight search weekly for the strangers

The alert covers people you know. The spotlight search covers people you do not: decision makers who just arrived at your target accounts. LinkedIn describes it as the **Job Changes** filter "within the Spotlights section" on its [how-to-use page](https://business.linkedin.com/sell/sales-navigator/how-to-use); older write-ups such as [Evaboot's filter guide](https://evaboot.com/blog/linkedin-sales-navigator-search-filters) call it "Changed jobs" and describe it as people who took a new position in the last 90 days. Same filter, different label depending on the version you are on.

Open **Lead filters** and build one saved search per segment:

| Filter | Value | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Account lists or Current company | `Watch: target accounts`, or your top 50 account names | Keeps the result to companies you would sell to anyway |
| Seniority level | Director, VP, CXO, Owner or Partner | The arrival that moves budget, not every new hire |
| Function | The function that buys what you sell | Removes unrelated departments |
| Spotlights | **Job Changes** | The trigger itself |
| Geography | Your territory | Optional, but it shrinks the list fast |

Save it as `New leaders at target accounts` with alerts on, and still run it by hand once a week: a saved-search alert tells you there are new results, and you want to catch an arrival in its first two or three weeks rather than at the end of the 90-day window.

For departures, open the **Account page** of each key account. LinkedIn's page says Account pages show "which decision-makers have joined or left", which is the fastest way to learn that the contact you relied on has gone, often before their profile says so.

## Step 5: Sweep once a month for the quiet movers

Some people update their title six weeks after they start. Some never post about it. The monthly sweep catches them with searches that need no alert.

**The Past company search (Sales Navigator).** Josh Asbury documented this in 2023 and the filters still exist: set **Past company** to one of your customers and set **Current company** to exclude that same company, so the results are people who used to work there and now work somewhere else. His run on one large customer returned [over 44,000 people, which a shared-connection filter cut to 156](https://www.joshasbury.com/posts/tracking-people-who-changed-jobs-with-linkedin/), so add a title, seniority or connection filter before you read anything. If your CRM is Salesforce and connected, the same post describes a system list, **Past Customers at New Companies**, that does this for closed-won contacts automatically.

**The Google query (free).** Two searches per key account, once a month:

```
"Acme Logistics" ("joins as" OR "appointed" OR "named" OR "promoted to") (VP OR director OR head)
site:linkedin.com/in "Acme Logistics" ("new position" OR "excited to share" OR "thrilled to join")
```

Getcleed's guide suggests the same idea as a standing Google Alert for ["[Company] hires" or "[Company] appoints"](https://www.getcleed.com/blog/job-change-sales-trigger), which is worth setting for your top twenty accounts so the sweep arrives in your inbox.

**The profile check (free, slow).** For the fifty names whose move would matter most, open each profile monthly and compare the headline to the sheet. Salesmotion estimates a quarterly check of [200 to 300 contacts at two to four hours](https://salesmotion.io/blog/champion-tracking-job-changes) and says a quarterly cadence catches moves about six weeks late; fifty names a month is under an hour and halves that delay.

This is the step where a tool earns its place, with a caveat about which kind. [Intentsly's LinkedIn signals](/features/linkedin-signals) read posts and engagement, so they catch the "excited to start as Head of Sales" announcement and the pain posts that follow a new role, and score each person against your ideal customer profile. They do not keep a job-change database, so keep the saved-lead alert and this sweep for the people who move without saying anything.

## What each surface sees and misses

| Surface | Who it sees | Who it misses | How late it runs |
|---|---|---|---|
| Catch Up tab and job-change notification (free) | First-degree connections who edit their profile | Non-connections; updates LinkedIn chooses not to surface | Days to weeks after the edit, per Salesmotion's description linked above |
| Sales Navigator lead alert | Saved leads who edit their profile | Anyone not saved | One to four weeks after the update, per CatchIntent's observation linked above |
| Job Changes spotlight search | Anyone matching your filters who changed jobs in the last 90 days | People who have not updated their profile yet | Same day you run it, limited by the profile edit |
| Past company search and Google query | Alumni of a named company; announced appointments | Moves with no profile edit and no announcement | Monthly, by design |

No row covers everyone, and every row depends on the person editing their profile or posting. That is why the routine uses four surfaces instead of one.

## Step 6: Log, score and message within the week

One sheet, one row per move:

```
date_spotted | name | cohort | old_company | old_title | new_company | new_title
| source (catch-up / sn-alert / sn-search / sweep) | score | message_sent | reply | next_step
```

Score each move on four yes-or-no questions, one point each:

1. **Fit.** Is the new company inside your ideal customer profile?
2. **Authority.** Does the new title own the budget or the workflow you sell into, or is it a lateral move or a wider title with the same scope?
3. **Relationship.** Did this person use your product or argue for it, or are they a stranger from the spotlight search?
4. **Recency.** Is the move under thirty days old by the profile's start date?

Four points: message today. Three: this week. Two: add to the normal outbound list. One or zero: log it and move on. Embers' playbook runs a [ten-point model reviewed within 48 hours](https://useembers.com/blog/job-change-buying-signal/); the four-question version exists so a sheet filter can sort the week in a minute.

The message depends on the cohort. Both templates are under 60 words so they survive on a phone:

```
Past champion, new company (score 4):
Congrats on the move to {new_company}, {first_name}. At {old_company} you pushed for
{product} because of {the problem it solved}. If {new_company} has the same gap, happy
to show you what has changed since, or just answer questions. No rush in week one.
```

```
New decision maker at a target account (score 3):
Hi {first_name}, saw you joined {new_company} as {new_title}. Most new {function} leads
we talk to spend the first month auditing {the workflow you sell into}. If useful, I can
share how two companies your size set it up. Either way, good luck with the first 90 days.
```

Send the first message inside a week of spotting the move. Follow up on day five to seven with something useful (a comparison, a checklist, a reaction to a post of theirs), and a last touch around day twenty. Then stop until a new signal appears. The [LinkedIn message templates for buying signals](/blog/linkedin-message-templates-for-buying-signals) page has the follow-up variants.

## A worked example (hypothetical)

Northwind Payroll (a labelled hypothetical company) sells payroll software to UK firms with 50 to 500 staff. Dana, its only seller, exports 180 names: 60 past users from closed-won accounts, 25 champions from lost deals and 95 finance directors at target firms. She is connected to 70 of them.

Week one. She switches on the job-changes notification, sends connection requests to 20 of the unconnected names with a one-line note, saves all 180 as leads in two Sales Navigator lists, builds the `New leaders at target firms` search (account list, Director and above, Function = Finance, Spotlights = Job Changes) and sets Google Alerts for her top 20 accounts. Setup takes an afternoon.

Week three, Tuesday triage, eighteen minutes. The Catch Up tab shows a former payroll manager at a customer now "Head of Finance Operations" at a 300-person logistics firm: fit, authority, relationship and recency all yes, score 4, message sent that morning. The lead alert shows a finance director at a target firm who moved to a 2,000-person bank: fit no, score 1, logged. The spotlight search returns three new finance directors at target firms: one started two weeks ago, score 3, message sent Thursday with a two-page comparison; two started ten weeks ago, score 2, added to the normal sequence.

Month one sweep, fifty minutes. The Past company search on her largest customer finds a former user now "Finance Director" at an in-profile firm who never posted about the move: score 4, and the message mentions the rollout they ran together. The counts in this example are illustrative, not measured.

## Where this goes wrong

1. **Congratulating and pitching in the same message.** The first touch to someone in week one is a short note: why you are writing, one useful thing, no ask. The ask goes in the follow-up. Their inbox is already full of vendors who did not wait.
2. **Scoring every move the same.** A lateral move, a wider title with the same scope and a contractor-to-permanent profile edit all fire the alert, and none of them resets a budget. The authority question in Step 6 exists so you do not message them like a new decision maker.
3. **Running the alert and skipping the sweep.** Alerts know only what you saved and fire only after a profile edit. Teams that rely on them alone find the champion who moved in March in September, after the new company has already bought. The monthly sweep is the part that feels optional and is not.

## What to do with the list

Treat the sheet as a queue. Every Monday, sort by score and date, work the fours and threes first, and move anything older than ninety days without a reply into the normal outbound list, where it is a warmer name than a cold one. Keep the source column honest: after two months it tells you which of the four surfaces produces the moves that turn into meetings, and you can drop the one that never does. Job changes pair well with hiring signals at the same account, and the guide on [tracking hiring signals for sales](/guides/track-hiring-signals-for-sales) runs on the same weekly rhythm.

Then pair each row with what the person posts. A mover who writes about the process they inherited is a better first message than a mover who stays quiet, and reading those posts is the part a signal tool can do for you. ## FAQ

### Does LinkedIn notify you when a connection changes jobs?

Yes, for first-degree connections. The Catch Up tab under My Network lists new jobs, and the Notifications settings have a Job changes switch under Network catch-up updates that sends the same updates to your notification feed or your phone. Both depend on the person editing their profile, and LinkedIn chooses which updates to show, so pair them with a monthly check of the names that matter most.

### Can you track job changes on LinkedIn without Sales Navigator?

Yes, if you are connected to the people and the list is small enough to check by hand. The free notification covers connections, a monthly Google query covers announced appointments at key accounts, and a profile check covers the fifty names you cannot afford to miss. Sales Navigator adds the saved-lead alert and the Job Changes spotlight for strangers at target accounts, which is where a free account runs out.

### How soon after a job change should you reach out?

Within a week of spotting it, with a short note and no pitch, then a useful follow-up a week later and a last touch around day twenty. The pages ranking for this topic treat the first ninety days as the window in which a new leader reviews vendors, and the early touches are the ones that get replies. After ninety days, treat the person as a warm name on the normal outbound list.

### How do I track past customers who moved to new companies?

Save them as leads in Sales Navigator so the Lead Changed Jobs alert fires, and run the Past company search monthly with the customer's name in Past company and excluded from Current company. If Salesforce is connected, the Past Customers at New Companies list does this for closed-won contacts. On a free account, connect with them and rely on the Catch Up tab plus a monthly profile check.

### Does the Sales Navigator Job Changes filter show every move?

No. It returns people who changed jobs in the last ninety days within the other filters you set, and it only knows about a move once the person updates their profile. Someone who starts in January and edits their headline in March appears in March. That delay is why the guide keeps a monthly sweep and a profile check next to the filter rather than trusting one source.

### Which Sales Navigator alerts should go to email?

The job-change and role-change alerts for saved leads, and nothing else. Those are the two you want the same day, and a daily summary email keeps them out of the noise of profile views and company news. Leave the rest in the product and read them during the weekly triage, so the inbox stays a place where an alert still means something.
