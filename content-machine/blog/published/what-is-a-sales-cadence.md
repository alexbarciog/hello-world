**A sales cadence is the planned series of contact attempts a seller makes to one prospect, with the channel, the message and the number of days between each attempt decided in advance.** Most answers to "what is a sales cadence" stop there and hand you a day-by-day template. The part the templates hide is the first design choice: what starts the clock. A cadence that starts on the day you picked a name from a list behaves very differently from one that starts on the day the prospect did something.

## Why does a sales cadence matter?

A cadence matters because it decides the two things a prospect notices about outbound: how often you turn up and whether each message adds anything. Without one, follow-up depends on a rep's memory, so most prospects get one message and then silence.

The follow-up numbers that circulate online point the same way, with limits worth stating. Woodpecker's analysis of its own users' campaigns puts the average reply rate at about 9% for campaigns with no follow-up and about 13% with at least one, rising to 16% and 27% for experienced senders; the post gives no sample size, so treat it as one vendor's customers rather than the market ([Woodpecker](https://woodpecker.co/blog/follow-up-statistics/)). RAIN Group's survey of 489 outbound sellers reports an average of 8 touches to get a first meeting, and 5 for the sellers it rates as top performers; that is what sellers said they did, not a measured reply curve ([RAIN Group](https://www.rainsalestraining.com/blog/how-many-touches-does-it-take-to-make-a-sale)). Both say the same plain thing: one message is not a cadence.

Take a labelled hypothetical. Marlowe Freight Software sells routing tools to 50-person logistics firms. Its founder emails a list of 200 operations managers on a Monday, hears nothing from 190 of them, and moves on. A cadence would have scheduled a LinkedIn connection request on Thursday, a second email a week later quoting something specific about the firm, and a last short note at day 14. Nothing about the list changed; the number of chances for a reply did.

## How does a sales cadence work?

A sales cadence is five decisions written down before the first message goes out. Every template online, however long, is a particular answer to these five.

| Decision | The question it answers | Typical answer in ranking templates |
|---|---|---|
| Entry | Who goes into the cadence, and what counts as day 1 | A name added from a list; day 1 is the day the rep adds it |
| Channels | Where each touch happens | Email, phone, LinkedIn, sometimes video or a comment on a post |
| Spacing | How many days between touches | 1 to 5 days early, longer later |
| Progression | What changes from one message to the next | New context, proof, a sharper question, a smaller ask |
| Exit | What ends the cadence | A reply, a clear no, or the last step (a "breakup" message) |

The templates agree on shape more than they admit. Pipedrive's five numbered examples run 6 to 8 touches over 8 to 28 days ([Pipedrive](https://pipedrive.com/en/blog/sales-cadence)). Cognism's 21-day example mixes LinkedIn, email, calls and video, and quotes a recommendation of 8 to 12 touches ([Cognism](https://www.cognism.com/blog/ultimate-b2b-prospecting-cadence)). HubSpot's version of the Sales Hacker cadence is about 9 touches over 10 days by email, InMail and phone ([HubSpot](https://blog.hubspot.com/sales/sales-cadence-example)). Woodpecker's cold version is email-heavy with one LinkedIn touch and a final close after day 14 to 17 ([Woodpecker](https://woodpecker.co/blog/sales-cadence/)). Pick any of them and you have a workable spacing and progression. None of them changes the entry row, which is the row that matters most.

### What starts the clock?

Every example cadence starts day 1 on the seller's calendar. The prospect's calendar is somewhere else. A calendar-started cadence sends the same five messages to someone who posted yesterday asking for recommendations and to someone who has not thought about the problem in a year. A signal-started cadence enters a prospect only when they do something observable, so day 1 is the day they posted, commented, changed jobs or started following a competitor, and the first message can quote it.

Here are the two side by side for Marlowe Freight Software, both hypothetical.

| | Calendar-started cadence | Signal-started cadence |
|---|---|---|
| Entry | Operations manager found in a list on Monday | Operations manager commented on a competitor's post about late deliveries |
| Day 1 | Email: who we are and what we do | LinkedIn connection request; the note quotes the comment |
| Day 3 | LinkedIn connection request | Message: one question about the late-delivery problem |
| Day 7 | Email: case study | Email: a short note with one relevant example, if no reply |
| Day 12 | Call or voicemail | Message: a last useful link, then stop |
| Day 14 | Breakup email | Already stopped |
| What the first line can say | Nothing about them | What they said, in their words |

The signal-started version is shorter because it needs fewer attempts to earn a reply: the prospect already raised the subject. On LinkedIn it is also bounded by the platform. Intentsly's own safety caps are 30 messages and 30 connection requests per account per day, with 15 recommended, and up to 2 LinkedIn accounts per plan, so a cadence that runs on LinkedIn has to be designed around a daily budget of roughly 15 new conversations rather than around how many names a list holds. That budget is why the entry row matters: if you can start 15 conversations a day, you want them to be with the 15 people who did something this week. Finding those people is the manual part; Intentsly watches [LinkedIn keyword posts, comments and competitor engagement](/features/linkedin-signals) and surfaces that day's prospects scored against your ideal customer profile, so the cadence starts from the signal instead of the spreadsheet.

One first-hand example, with its limits. When Intentsly launched we ran one signal-started cadence on our own product: founders posting complaints about cold outreach burnout entered it, the AI SDR sent the LinkedIn connection requests and messages and held the conversation, and demos were booked by the system. The result was 14 qualified demos in 14 days with zero manual outreach and a reply rate 4x our previous cold campaigns ([case study](/case-studies)). That is one company over two weeks, so it shows the entry rule worked for us and says nothing about your market.

## How many touches should a sales cadence have?

Six to eight touches across two to three weeks is where the tracked data and the surveys land, and the right number depends more on who entered the cadence than on the template. Three sources are quoted on nearly every ranking page. Here is what each one actually measured, because the limits change how you read the number.

| Source | What it says | What it measured | Limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| [Yesware](https://www.yesware.com/blog/sales-cadence/) | Best cadence was 6 touches over about two weeks, mixing calls and emails 1 to 5 days apart | 33 million tracked email activities from eight technology companies | Phone and email only; only the first six touches of each campaign were analysed, so it could not find an optimum above six |
| [Woodpecker](https://woodpecker.co/blog/follow-up-statistics/) | 2 to 3 follow-ups is the useful range; the fifth and later add little | Woodpecker users' cold email campaigns | No sample size given; email only |
| [RAIN Group](https://www.rainsalestraining.com/blog/how-many-touches-does-it-take-to-make-a-sale) | 8 touches on average to get a first meeting, 5 for top performers | Survey of 489 sellers who prospect outbound | Self-reported, not tracked |

Read together: the only tracked study capped itself at six touches, the email data says returns fall after the third follow-up, and the eight-touch figure is what sellers remember doing. A reasonable default is five to seven touches over two to three weeks, cut shorter when the prospect entered on a signal you can quote in the first line.

## Sales cadence vs sales sequence vs sales process vs follow-up

The ranking pages use cadence and sequence as synonyms, and most teams can too. Keeping the four words apart still helps when you design one.

| Term | What it is | Scope | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sales cadence | The timing and rhythm of attempts to reach one prospect | One prospect, two to four weeks | "Connection request, a message two days later, an email at day 7" |
| Sales sequence | The ordered list of steps a tool runs; in practice the same thing | One prospect, the same span | The same cadence saved in your outreach tool |
| Follow-up | Any attempt after the first | One step inside a cadence | The day 7 email |
| Sales process | Every stage from first contact to a closed deal | One deal, weeks to months | Prospecting, discovery, proposal, negotiation, close |

A cadence lives inside the prospecting stage of the process and ends the moment a conversation starts. The lesson on [what cold outreach is](/learn/what-is-cold-outreach) covers the first message in more depth; the lesson on [what a buying signal is](/learn/what-is-a-buying-signal) covers what qualifies as an entry event.

## How to use a sales cadence this week

Three actions, each under an hour.

1. **Write your cadence as the five rows.** Entry, channels, spacing, progression, exit, on one page. If the entry row says "added from a list", write a second version where it says "did X on LinkedIn" and decide which one you will run. The [guide to setting up signal-based outbound](/guides/set-up-signal-based-outbound) walks through building the entry rule.
2. **Fix the stop rules before the first message.** A cadence stops on a reply (move to conversation), a clear no (stop and note the reason), a bounced or wrong contact (stop), or the last step (stop, and revisit only when a new signal appears). Write all four down so that stopping follows a rule rather than a feeling.
3. **Run it on 15 people and count.** Enter 15 prospects on the same day, run every step, and count replies by step. After two weeks you know whether your third and fourth touches earn anything, which tells you how long your cadence needs to be in your market, rather than in Yesware's eight companies. The [guide to writing a LinkedIn outreach message](/guides/write-linkedin-outreach-message) gives first-line patterns for each entry event.

## FAQ

### What is an example of a sales cadence?

A six-touch outbound cadence over two weeks: day 1 a LinkedIn connection request with a note, day 3 a short message with one question, day 7 an email with one relevant example, day 10 a comment on something the prospect posted, day 14 a last short message saying you will stop. Every step adds something new, and none repeats the first message.

### How many touches should a sales cadence have?

Five to seven over two to three weeks is a sound default. The tracked data that exists found its best cadence at six touches but only analysed the first six, the email data shows returns falling after the third follow-up, and the common eight-touch figure comes from a survey of sellers. Shorten the cadence when the prospect entered on a signal you can quote.

### What is the difference between a sales cadence and a sales sequence?

In practice none; the vendor pages that rank for both terms use them interchangeably. Strictly, cadence describes the rhythm and timing of attempts to reach a prospect, while sequence describes the ordered list of steps a tool executes. If your team already has one word for the saved series of touches, keep it and spend the time on the entry and stop rules instead.

### Should a sales cadence include LinkedIn?

Yes for B2B, with two cautions. LinkedIn is where a prospect's recent activity is visible, which gives the first line something to quote, and connection requests and messages are read in a different place from email. The platform limits volume, so plan around a small daily number of new conversations rather than a long list, and never send the same text on both channels.

### When should a sales cadence stop?

On a reply, a clear no, a wrong contact, or the last planned step, whichever comes first. Write those four rules down before the first message goes out. After the last step, do not keep extending the cadence; note the prospect and re-enter them only when something new happens on their side, such as a post, a job change or a comment on a competitor's page.
