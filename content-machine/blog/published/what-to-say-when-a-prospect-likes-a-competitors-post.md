> [!SUMMARY]
> - Say something about the topic of the post, never about the like and never about the competitor.
> - Run a 30-second check first: right role, right company, not a competitor employee, post was about your problem. Fail any one and you connect without a note or skip.
> - Four scripts below, one per kind of post: a problem post, a launch post, a repeat liker, a like plus a second signal. All under 300 characters.
> - Expect a like to convert worse than a comment. Judge it against a measured baseline, not a vendor's homepage.

What to say when a prospect likes a competitor's post: nothing about the like, nothing about the competitor, one line about the topic of the post and one question about whether the problem is live for them. A like is the weakest signal you can see on LinkedIn, so the message asks less than a comment-triggered one would: to connect, or one question, with an easy way to say "not now". The scripts below cover the four situations a like turns up in.

## Is a like worth a message at all?

Sometimes, and less often than the tools that sell you the list suggest. A like takes one tap and says "this topic is mine" and not much else. Unify's own guide to this play ranks a like last among engagement types, after comments and reposts ([Unify](https://unifygtm.com/use-cases/turn-social-engagement-into-pipeline)), and Koka Sexton calls a single like "noise" until it stacks with other signals ([Koka Sexton](https://kokasexton.com/linkedin-buyer-engagement-signals/)). Both are right about the strength. The reason to message anyway is timing: the person is thinking about your problem this week, and most of your market is not. LinkedIn's B2B Institute puts it at 95% of potential buyers not being ready to buy today ([LinkedIn B2B Institute, 95-5 rule](https://business.linkedin.com/marketing-solutions/b2b-institute/b2b-research/trends/95-5-rule)). A like does not move someone into the 5%. It tells you which of the 95% are at least reading.

There is a louder view. Carta's Peter Walker wrote in January 2026 that nobody has ever bought anything from cold outreach that opens with a LinkedIn post reaction, and that the rest of such an email is as useless as its subject line ([Carta](https://carta.com/data/linkedin-cold-outreach-linkedin-post-reaction-useless/)). He is describing the "I saw you liked X's post" email, and on that he is right: a message that leads with the like is a message about your tracking, not about the reader. The scripts below exist to never be that email.

## The 30-second check before you write anything

Run this on the profile before you open the message box. Fail one and the like goes to the watch list, not the inbox.

1. **Role.** Does this person own or feel the problem you solve? A like from a founder at a 40-person company counts. A like from an intern, a student or a job seeker does not, and competitor posts attract all three.
2. **Company.** Does the company fit your [ideal customer profile](/learn/what-is-an-ideal-customer-profile) on size and type? If you would not add them to a list by hand, a like does not change that.
3. **Side.** Do they work for the competitor, a partner of the competitor, an agency in your category or a vendor selling to the same buyer? Read the headline. These people like competitor posts for a living.
4. **Post.** Was the post about the problem you solve, or was it a hiring announcement, a personal story or a launch? The script depends on the answer, and a like on a personal story is not a signal at all.
5. **Recency.** Did the like happen in the last three days? Older than that and you are no longer "this week" in their head.

Passing all five gets a message. Passing four, with the only miss on recency or post type, gets a connection request with no note, so the person turns up in your feed and you see their next move. Anything else is a skip. The [three ways to track competitor post engagers](/blog/track-competitor-linkedin-post-engagers) cover how to get the names in the first place; this page is about what happens after the check.

> [!WARNING]
> Never write "I saw you liked", "noticed you engaged with" or the competitor's name in a first message. The reader learns two things: that you watch their activity, and who your competitor is. Neither helps you. Lead with the topic, which you know because you read the post.

## What to say when a prospect likes a competitor's post about your problem

This is the best case: the post described a pain you fix, and a qualified person tapped like. Treat the like as proof of topic, not of intent. The connection note names the topic and offers something; the first message asks whether the problem is live and gives an exit.

**Connection note (144 characters)**

```message
The thread on reply rates dropping after the third follow-up is one I keep seeing from [role] teams. I work on exactly that. Open to connecting?
```

**First message after they accept (238 characters)**

```message
Thanks for connecting. One question: is reply rate on follow-ups something you are dealing with now, or just following the conversation? If now, I have a one-page note on the two changes that moved it for us. If just following, ignore me.
```

Replace "reply rates dropping after the third follow-up" with the topic of the post in the words the author used, and "[role]" with the reader's role. Do not replace the exit line. "If just following, ignore me" is what makes the message safe to send to a weak signal, because it costs the reader nothing to say no.

**If they reply "just following" (113 characters)**

```message
Fair enough, thanks for saying so. I will post what we find on it over the next month, so no need to do anything.
```

That reply is not a loss. You now have a connection who told you the topic matters to them and when it does not. The next signal from this person, a post or a comment or a job change, gets the stronger [message template for that signal](/blog/linkedin-message-templates-for-buying-signals) instead of this one.

## What to say when the post was a product launch or announcement

Launch posts collect likes from customers, friends, employees and peers congratulating the founder, and only a few from buyers comparing options. Valley's playbook for this case leads with the category rather than the launch and excludes staff and partners before sending ([Valley](https://www.joinvalley.co/blog/competitor-launch-post-engagers-playbook)). The script here does the same: it asks a category question and never mentions the product that launched.

**Connection note (175 characters)**

```message
A lot of [role] teams I talk to are rethinking how they find buyers this quarter rather than buying bigger lists. Curious whether that is on your desk too. Open to connecting?
```

**First message (238 characters)**

```message
Thanks for connecting. Are you actively comparing ways to [category outcome] right now, or is that further out? If now, happy to share where the options differ, including where ours is the wrong fit. If further out, I will leave it there.
```

"Including where ours is the wrong fit" is doing real work. A person who just liked a competitor's launch has a candidate in mind. A message that admits a limit reads as a comparison, not a pitch, and a comparison is what they are already doing.

> [!EXAMPLE]
> A competitor posts "We just shipped multi-account sending." A sales lead at a 30-person agency likes it. Your check passes. You do not write about multi-account sending. You write about how they find buyers today and whether they are comparing options, then let them bring up the feature if it matters.

## What to say to a repeat liker

Someone who likes three or more competitor or industry posts on your topic inside a month has moved from "this is my topic" to "I am reading everything on this". That is still not a comment, but the pattern earns a direct question, and the note can skip the warm-up.

**Connection note (180 characters)**

```message
You and I follow the same corner of LinkedIn: everything on finding buyers from what people post rather than from lists. I work with [type of company] on it. Worth comparing notes?
```

**First message (221 characters)**

```message
Thanks for connecting. Direct question, feel free to ignore it: are you evaluating a way to [outcome] in the next month or two? If yes, I will send a two-line summary of what we do and you can decide. If no, no follow-up.
```

Counting repeat likes by hand means checking the reaction lists of several accounts every few days, which is the part that stops happening after week two. Intentsly tracks who engages with the competitor and industry posts you name, scores each person against your ideal customer and queues the ones worth a message, so the [LinkedIn signals feed](/features/linkedin-signals) does the watching and you send the two lines.

## What to say when the like comes with a second signal

A like plus anything stronger stops being a like. If the same person also commented on a post about the problem, started a new role in the last month, or works at a company hiring for the role you help, write to the stronger signal and ignore the like completely. The like confirms the topic; the second signal gives you the sentence.

| What you saw | Which signal leads | What the first line is about | Send within |
|---|---|---|---|
| Like on a problem post only | The like | The topic of the post | 3 days |
| Like plus a comment on any post about the problem | The comment | The point they made in their comment | 2 days |
| Like plus a new role in the last 30 days | The job change | What the first 90 days in that role usually surface | 2 to 4 weeks after the start |
| Like plus their company hiring for the role you help | The hiring signal | The capacity gap the job post implies | While the post is open |
| Like on a launch post, nothing else | The like, launch version | The category decision, not the product | 3 days |
| Like on a personal or hiring post | Nothing | Skip, or connect with no note | n/a |

The scripts for the comment, job change and hiring cases are in the [seven-sequence template set](/blog/linkedin-message-templates-for-buying-signals), each with its own do-not-send rule. The [buying signal lesson](/learn/what-is-a-buying-signal) has the one-minute test for whether what you saw counts as a signal at all.

## Phrases that make the message worse

Most of these appear in at least one of the templates or playbooks ranking for this query today, and each one costs replies.

- **"I saw you liked" or "noticed your engagement with".** This is the surveillance opener. The reader did one tap and now knows you logged it.
- **The competitor's name or handle.** One ranking template opens with "I'm following [competitor handle or employee] and saw you ..." ([Common Room](https://commonroom.io/prompts/linkedin-engagement-with-competitors-post)). That puts your competitor's name in your first line and invites the reader to go check them out instead.
- **"We're better than X."** The person liked X. You have just told them their judgement is wrong, in your opening.
- **A pitch in the connection note.** The note has one job, which is to get accepted. The product can wait for the first message, and even there it is one clause.
- **A second follow-up.** A like earns one note, one message and at most one follow-up. After that you are messaging someone who has now ignored you three times, from a weak signal.

> [!TIP]
> Fuzzy's test is the one to keep: if the person could see exactly how you found them, the message should still read as reasonable ([Fuzzy AI](https://getfuzzy.ai/blog/competitor-followers-linkedin-prospecting)). Every script on this page passes that test because it is about the topic, and the topic is public.

## What reply rate should a like-triggered message get?

Lower than a comment-triggered one, and you should measure it separately. The most recent large baseline is Expandi's benchmark of 13,218,869 connection requests and 6,730,447 messages across 13,302 accounts, sent between May 2025 and April 2026, which puts the platform-wide average at 28.5% connection acceptance, a 3.0% reply rate to connection notes and 10.4% to messages, with the note reply rate drifting from 3.5% to 2.2% over the year ([Expandi, LinkedIn Outreach Benchmarks 2026](https://expandi.io/blog/linkedin-outreach-benchmarks-2026/)). Those are automation averages across every kind of outreach. A targeted message to a qualified liker should beat them; a message that opens with the like usually does not.

```stats
28.5% | average connection acceptance across 13.2 million requests, May 2025 to April 2026 | https://expandi.io/blog/linkedin-outreach-benchmarks-2026/
3.0% | average reply rate to connection notes in the same dataset | https://expandi.io/blog/linkedin-outreach-benchmarks-2026/
10.4% | average reply rate to messages in the same dataset | https://expandi.io/blog/linkedin-outreach-benchmarks-2026/
```

We do not have a first-hand number for like-triggered messages on their own, so we will not quote one. Our one campaign-level result is from launching Intentsly: we pointed our own signal tracking and AI SDR at founders posting about cold outreach burnout, let the AI hold the conversations, and booked 14 qualified demos in 14 days with no manual outreach, at a reply rate 4x our previous cold campaigns. Most of those leads came from intent signals caught in real time. The [case study](/case-studies) has the account; it does not break the leads down by signal type, so we cannot tell you what share came from likes alone.

Whatever you send, log the signal type next to each name and read the reply rate per type after a month. Sending at the caps we run, 15 messages and 15 connection requests a day per account with 30 as the hard maximum and two accounts per workspace, gives you enough replies in that month to see whether likes deserve their slot. The [reply-rate lesson](/learn/good-reply-rate-for-linkedin-outreach) has the ranges to judge against.

## What to do this week

Pick one competitor whose posts are about your problem, not about themselves. Open their last five posts, run the 30-second check on every liker, and send the problem-post script to the ones who pass, 15 a day at most. Log each name with the signal and the script used. After two weeks you will know whether likes in your market are worth the slot or whether comments should get all of it.

## FAQ

### Should I mention that they liked the competitor's post?

No. Mentioning the like tells the reader you are tracking their activity, and mentioning the competitor puts another vendor's name in your opening line. Write about the topic of the post instead, in the words the author used. You know the topic because you read the post, which is a normal thing to have done, and the reader does not need to know how you got to their profile.

### How soon after the like should I send the message?

Within three days. A like is a small act and the topic leaves the person's head quickly, so a message a week later has lost the timing advantage that made the like worth anything. If you missed the window, do not message; connect without a note so the person appears in your feed, and wait for the next post, comment or job change before you write.

### Is a like actually a buying signal?

It is a topic signal, not a buying signal. It tells you the person reads about the problem you solve, which is useful for timing and nothing more. A comment, a question in the thread, a new role or a hiring post show a need or a change. Treat a like as the reason to look at the profile, and let the profile decide whether you write.

### What if the person is already the competitor's customer?

You often cannot tell from a like, which is one more reason the scripts ask a question instead of pitching. If they reply that they already use the competitor, thank them, ask what they would change about it, and leave the thread there. That answer is worth more than a demo request, and the person will see your posts when the renewal comes round.

### Can I automate messaging people who liked a competitor's post?

Automate the finding and the scoring, and keep the sending slow and inside LinkedIn's limits. We cap our own accounts at 30 messages and 30 connection requests a day and recommend 15, with up to two LinkedIn accounts per workspace. Sending from a browser extension that scrapes reaction lists is what gets accounts restricted; the scripts here are short enough to send by hand at that volume.

### How many times should I follow up on a like?

Once at most, and for the weakest case not at all. The sequence for a like is a connection note, a first message after acceptance, and one short follow-up five or six days later only if the first message asked a question. If they said "just following", there is no follow-up; the next contact comes from them or from their next signal.
