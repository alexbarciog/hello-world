**Signal-based selling is a way of running outbound in which you contact a person because of something they or their company just did, and you say so in the first line, instead of working through a list in order.** The "something" is a signal: a public action or change that raises the odds that this buyer has your problem now. The short answer to "what is signal-based selling" is a rule about timing and wording: reach out when there is a reason, and lead with the reason.

## Why does signal-based selling matter?

It matters because most of the people on any list you build are not buying, and a list cannot tell you which ones are. The Ehrenberg-Bass Institute's work for the LinkedIn B2B Institute puts it at up to 95% of businesses not in the market for most goods and services at any one time, reasoning from switching cycles of around five years ([Ehrenberg-Bass summary](https://marketingscience.info/ehrenberg-bass-95-of-b2b-buyers-are-not-in-the-market-for-your-products/)). A signal is how you find the other 5% without messaging all of them. Buyers also do more of the early work alone: in a Gartner survey of 646 B2B buyers fielded in late 2025, 67% said they prefer to buy without a sales rep involved, and Gartner notes they search and vet suppliers "with fewer early interactions" ([Gartner, 9 March 2026](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2026-03-09-gartner-sales-survey-finds-67-percent-of-b2b-buyers-prefer-a-rep-free-experience)). A signal is often the only trace of that research you will ever see.

Take a hypothetical example. Harbor Analytics, a 25-person software company, sells reporting tools to finance teams. Its sales lead has a list of 400 controllers. Working it top to bottom, each message opens with who Harbor is. One morning a controller at a target company comments under a LinkedIn post: "We've outgrown spreadsheets for month-end, what are people using?" That comment is a signal. The message that quotes it in line one and answers the question is signal-based selling. The other 399 messages that week are not.

## What counts as a signal, and what does not?

A signal is an observable action by a named person, or a change at a named company, that you can link to and that changes what you would write. Most of what a sales tool calls a signal fails that test. Here is the two-question version you can apply to any data point in a minute.

| Question | Passes | Fails |
|---|---|---|
| Can I name the person or company and point at what they did? | A comment, a post, a job-change announcement, a hiring post, a funding announcement, a like on a competitor's post | "Accounts in your territory showing interest in your category" with no name attached |
| Does it change the first line of my message? | "You asked yesterday what people use for month-end close" | "I noticed your company is growing" (true of every company you would message anyway) |

A page view with no name is activity, not a signal; Common Room makes the same distinction in its own definition ([Common Room, 28 August 2026](https://www.commonroom.io/blog/signal-based-selling-definition/)). A company matching your ideal customer profile is fit, not a signal; the lesson on [what an ideal customer profile is](/learn/what-is-an-ideal-customer-profile) covers that side. Fit tells you whether to ever contact someone. A signal tells you whether to contact them this week.

The common signal types, which every ranking page lists in some order, fall into four groups. Things people say in public: a question, a complaint about a tool, a post about a pain point. Things people do in public: liking or commenting on a competitor's post, following a competitor's page, engaging with an industry leader. Changes in who decides: a new role, a champion moving company, a first sales or marketing hire. Changes in the company's situation: a funding round, an acquisition, a hiring wave, a tool being dropped. The first two groups name a person and are the strongest for a first message. The last two name a company and are covered in the lesson on [what a trigger event is](/learn/what-is-a-trigger-event-in-sales).

## Signal-based selling vs intent data vs trigger events vs account-based selling

The four terms overlap, and vendors use them loosely. The differences that matter are what each one names and what it asks you to do next.

| | Signal-based selling | Intent data | Trigger-event selling | Account-based selling |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| What it is | A method: act on a person's or company's recent action, and quote it | A data product: aggregated research activity, usually at company level | A method: act on changes in a company's situation | A method: choose a fixed set of accounts and work all of them |
| Names | A person, usually | A company, usually | A company | A list |
| Timing comes from | The action itself, today | Topic scores that rise over weeks | The event | Your plan |
| First line of the message | Quotes what they did | Cannot quote anything specific | Quotes the change | Quotes your research on the account |
| Fails when | You cannot see the action | The score names no one to contact | The change created no need | Nobody on the list has a reason to buy this quarter |

Intent data is one possible input to signal-based selling, which the lesson on [what intent data is](/learn/what-is-intent-data) explains. Trigger-event selling is signal-based selling restricted to company-level changes. Account-based selling is the opposite order of operations: you pick the companies first and look for reasons second, which works for large deals with long cycles and few buyers.

## Do the numbers quoted for signal-based selling hold up?

Mostly no, and you should know that before you build a forecast on them. The pages ranking for this term quote reply-rate lifts of two to four times, three to five times, and five to ten times; reply rates of 15 to 25 percent against a cold baseline of 3 to 5 percent; and close rates of 33 to 41 percent against 18 to 25 percent. The figures do not agree with each other, and when a source is given it is a vendor's own benchmark report, a LinkedIn post, or, as [Abmatic's guide](https://abmatic.ai/blog/what-is-signal-based-selling-2026) states openly, "practitioner reports" on Reddit and LinkedIn. None traces to a study you can read with a method and a sample.

The honest claim is narrower. A message that quotes something the reader did is harder to ignore than one that does not, so reply rates go up when you switch. By how much depends on your market, your message and how fast you reply. The number worth trusting is your own reply rate before and after, over the same four weeks.

## How to use signal-based selling this week

Three actions, each under an hour, and none needs a tool.

1. **Write down two signals you can see.** Pick one thing people say and one thing people do, for the role you sell to. Examples: a post asking which tool to use, and a comment on a competitor's announcement. Write one sentence per signal: what it looks like, and what your first line will be when you see it.
2. **Run two LinkedIn searches a day for five days.** Use the Posts tab, sorted by Latest. For things people say: `"anyone using" OR "what are people using" <your category>`. For complaints: `"switching from" <competitor>` or `"frustrated with" <tool>`. For things people do: open the competitor's last three posts and read the commenters' titles. Keep a plain list: name, company, the signal, the link, the date. After five days you know how many signals a week your market produces, which is the number that decides whether you need software.
3. **Send one message per signal, the same day.** First line quotes the signal in the person's words. Second line says why that usually leads to the problem you solve. Third line asks one question. ## FAQ

### What is a signal in sales?

A signal is an observable action by a named person, or a change at a named company, that raises the chance they have the problem you solve right now. A comment asking which tool to use, a like on a competitor's post, a new head of sales starting, and a funding round all count. A page view with no name, or a company that merely fits your profile, does not.

### Is signal-based selling just lead scoring with a new name?

No, although some tools present it that way. Lead scoring adds up points from fit and past activity to rank a list, and the rank is stable from week to week. Signal-based selling starts from a single recent action, acts on it within days, and uses the action itself as the opening line. Scoring can help you decide which signals to act on first; it does not replace the signal.

### Do you need a tool for signal-based selling?

Not to start. Two LinkedIn searches a day, a competitor's comment section and a spreadsheet are enough to run it for a week and count how many signals your market produces. A tool earns its place when the daily search takes longer than the messaging, or when you need to watch more sources, people or languages than one person can read.

### What is the difference between signal-based selling and intent data?

Intent data is a product: a score that says a company has been researching a topic, usually without naming anyone there. Signal-based selling is a method: contact a person because of something specific they did, and quote it. Intent data can feed the method, but a score alone gives you no first line, because there is nothing specific to refer to.

### How fast do you have to act on a signal?

The same day for things people say, because a question or a complaint is answered within hours and the asker moves on. Within the week for things people do, such as engaging with a competitor's post. Within the quarter for company changes such as a new leader, who reviews inherited tools in the first months. If you cannot act inside those windows, the signal has become background information.

### What are examples of signal-based selling?

A sales lead sees a controller ask under a post which month-end tool to use and replies with an answer and one question. A founder notices who commented on a competitor's pricing-change announcement and messages the three whose titles match her profile. A recruiter sees a company post its first two sales vacancies and writes to the hiring manager about ramp time. In each case the message opens with the thing the person did.
