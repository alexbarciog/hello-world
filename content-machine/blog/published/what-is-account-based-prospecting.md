**Account-based prospecting is the practice of choosing a short list of companies that fit your ideal customer first, then finding and preparing to contact the people inside each one, instead of collecting individual leads wherever they appear.** The short answer to "what is account-based prospecting" is: company first, people second, outreach third. It is the finding stage of account-based selling, and it only pays off when deals are large enough to justify studying one company at a time.

## What is account-based prospecting in plain terms?

In plain terms, account-based prospecting flips the order of a normal prospect list. Lead-based prospecting starts with a person who matches a title and hopes the company fits. Account-based prospecting starts with a company that is known to fit and then works out who inside it owns the problem, who signs, and who will block.

Three things happen in the account-based version that do not happen in the lead-based one. The team agrees on a finite list of target accounts, grouped into tiers by how much each is worth. Each account gets its own research: what the company sells, what changed recently, who reports to whom. And outreach goes to several people at the same company in a coordinated way, rather than to one person each across hundreds of companies.

The vendor pages that rank for this term agree on that core. Woodpecker describes it as a method that starts with the company, not the individual contact ([Woodpecker, July 2026](https://woodpecker.co/blog/account-based-prospecting/)). Skylead draws the line at outreach: prospecting is the finding, researching and qualifying that happens before the first message, and selling is what happens after ([Skylead, updated September 2026](https://skylead.io/blog/account-based-prospecting/)). That boundary is the one this lesson uses.

## Why does account-based prospecting matter?

It matters because it changes what a seller spends time on. In a lead-based process, time goes into volume: more names, more sequences, more sends. In an account-based process, time goes into depth: understanding one company well enough that the first message could only have been written to them.

A hypothetical example shows the difference. Northgate Payroll, an invented 40-person software company, sells to mid-sized logistics firms. Its one SDR used to export 500 operations managers a month and send the same four-step sequence. Switching to account-based prospecting, she picks 25 logistics companies that match the ideal profile, maps three people in each (head of operations, finance lead, the dispatcher who feels the pain daily), and writes to all three with a message that mentions a route expansion the company announced. Fewer sends, but each one lands with context the previous version never had.

The cost is real and the ranking pages tend to skip it. Twenty-five accounts researched properly is a week of work before anything is sent. That is why the fit test below exists: the method is right for some teams and wrong for others, and the difference is mostly deal size and how many people have to agree.

## How does account-based prospecting work?

Account-based prospecting works in four stages, and every ranking playbook describes some version of them.

1. **Define the ideal customer profile.** Industry, size, geography, tech stack, and the situation that makes a company need you now. The lesson on [what an ideal customer profile is](/learn/what-is-an-ideal-customer-profile) has a worksheet for this.
2. **Build and tier the target account list.** A finite list, not a database export. Tier one gets a custom plan per account, tier two a lighter touch, tier three a shared sequence. The guide on [how to build a B2B prospect list](/guides/build-b2b-prospect-list) covers the sources and the template.
3. **Map the buying group in each account.** Who owns the problem, who controls budget, who will use the product, who could veto. Several of the ranking pages point at a Gartner page for the typical size of a B2B buying group; this lesson could not open that page during research and does not repeat its figure.
4. **Prepare the account for outreach.** Verified contact details, a reason to write that is specific to this company this month, and one message per role rather than one message for everyone.

The weak point in most playbooks is stage four. They build the list from firmographics, which tells you a company could buy, and then assign every account the same cadence, which ignores whether anyone there is ready. The cheapest fix is to watch what people inside the target accounts say in public. A head of operations at a tier-two account posting "our dispatch tool just doubled its price" moves that account to the top of the week; the lesson on [what a buying signal is](/learn/what-is-a-buying-signal) explains why. If you would rather not read LinkedIn all day, [Intentsly's LinkedIn signal monitoring](/features/linkedin-signals) watches keyword posts and competitor engagement for you and scores each person against your profile, so the week's accounts pick themselves.

## Account-based prospecting vs account-based marketing, selling and lead-based prospecting

The four terms overlap, and the pages that rank use them loosely. This table draws the lines the way the clearer sources do.

| Term | Who runs it | Unit of work | Starts | Ends | Line drawn from |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Account-based prospecting (ABP) | Sales (SDRs) | A named company | ICP agreed | First conversation booked | [Skylead](https://skylead.io/blog/account-based-prospecting/) |
| Account-based selling (ABS) | Sales (AEs) | A named company | First conversation | Closed deal, renewal | [Skylead](https://skylead.io/blog/account-based-prospecting/), [Pipedrive, Oct 2026](https://www.pipedrive.com/en/blog/account-based-sales) |
| Account-based marketing (ABM) | Marketing | A named company | List agreed with sales | Account engaged, handed to sales | [Tomba, June 2026](https://tomba.io/blog/account-based-prospecting) |
| Lead-based prospecting | Sales (SDRs) | An individual person | Title filter | Reply from anyone | [Woodpecker](https://woodpecker.co/blog/account-based-prospecting/) |

Two distinctions matter most. Prospecting versus selling is a question of stage: prospecting finds and prepares, selling runs the deal. Account-based versus lead-based is a question of unit: the company or the person. Tomba's framing is useful here: marketing provides the "air cover" of content and ads aimed at the list, prospecting is the "ground game" of SDRs working account by account.

## Does account-based prospecting fit your team?

It fits when a deal is large enough that several people at the buyer have to agree and the market is small enough that you can name the companies in it. Pipedrive's page is the one ranking source that states the reverse plainly: for low-value, high-volume sales, traditional prospecting is usually more efficient, and very short cycles or single-decision-maker purchases rarely justify the coordination ([Pipedrive](https://www.pipedrive.com/en/blog/account-based-sales)).

Answer these six questions with yes or no.

| Question | Yes means |
|---|---|
| Do two or more people at the buyer usually have to agree before they buy? | Account-based |
| Could you list every company you want as a customer in the next year, by name? | Account-based |
| Is one closed deal worth more than a week of a seller's time? | Account-based |
| Does the sales cycle usually run longer than a month? | Account-based |
| Do your best customers share an industry or a situation, not just a job title? | Account-based |
| Would losing a target account to a competitor hurt more than missing a hundred cold leads? | Account-based |

Four or more yes answers: run account-based prospecting on your tier-one list and keep lead-based for the rest. Two or three: pick ten accounts and test for one quarter before changing the whole process. One or none: your buyers are individuals making quick decisions, and the lesson on [what outbound sales is](/learn/what-is-outbound-sales) is the better place to start.

One first-hand note, with its limits stated. When Intentsly launched, we did not start from a target account list. We watched LinkedIn for founders complaining about cold outreach burnout and let the AI SDR handle the replies. The result was 14 qualified demos in 14 days with zero manual outreach ([case study](/case-studies)). That was person-first, signal-led prospecting rather than account-based prospecting, and it is one company over two weeks. The lesson for an account-based team is the combination: keep the named list, but let public signals decide which account gets the attention this week.

## How to use account-based prospecting this week

Three actions, each under an hour.

1. **Write the list of twenty.** Open your CRM, sort closed deals by value, and write down the twenty companies most like the top five. Those are your tier one. If you cannot fill twenty, that is a sign the market is small enough for the method.
2. **Map one account completely.** Pick the best one and find the three people who would be in the room for the purchase. The guide on [how to find decision makers on LinkedIn](/guides/find-decision-makers-on-linkedin) has the search strings. Write down what each one would need to hear.
3. **Set one signal watch.** For the twenty accounts, save a LinkedIn search on the company name plus the word your product replaces, and check it on Monday. Any account where someone posted about the problem moves to the front of the queue. ## FAQ

### What is the difference between account-based prospecting and account-based marketing?

Account-based prospecting is run by sales and ends when a conversation is booked with someone at a target account. Account-based marketing is run by marketing and uses content, ads and events aimed at the same accounts to make that conversation easier to get. Both start from one shared list of named companies; they differ in who does the work and where it stops.

### Is account-based prospecting the same as account-based selling?

No, although many pages use the terms interchangeably. Prospecting is the finding stage: choosing accounts, researching them, mapping the buying group and preparing the first message. Selling is everything after the first conversation: discovery, proposals, multi-threading the deal and closing. A team can do account-based selling with lead-based prospecting, but the handover is messier.

### How many accounts should one person work at once?

On LinkedIn the sending limit sets the number. Intentsly recommends 15 messages per LinkedIn account per day, which is 75 in a working week. If each account needs three people contacted with a first message and one follow-up, that is six messages per account, so one sender can open about twelve new accounts a week. Start with twenty in tier one and add more once those are worked.

### Does account-based prospecting work for small companies and small deals?

It works for small companies selling large deals, and not for anyone selling small ones. The fit test above is about deal size, buying-group size and cycle length, not the seller's headcount. A two-person firm selling a five-figure annual contract to a committee is a good fit; a fifty-person team selling a self-serve monthly plan to one user is not.

### What tools do you need for account-based prospecting?

A CRM that can hold accounts and contacts separately, a way to find and verify the people in each account, and a way to see when something changes at a target company. The first two can be LinkedIn plus a spreadsheet when the list is short. The third is where tools earn their keep, because reading twenty company pages every Monday is the step people stop doing first.
