**Multithreading in sales is the practice of building relationships with several people inside the buying company at the same time, so the deal does not depend on one contact.** That is the short answer to "what is multithreading in sales": several threads instead of one, so if one goes quiet the deal keeps moving. The opposite is single-threading, where one contact carries everything. Below: why it matters, how many threads a deal needs at each company size, and how to add one without annoying the person who let you in.

## Why does multithreading matter in a B2B deal?

It matters because the person you are talking to rarely decides alone, and sometimes stops being able to decide at all. A champion changes jobs, goes on leave, loses the budget line or simply stops replying. In a single-threaded deal, that is the end of the deal, and you usually find out weeks later.

The data on closed deals points the same way. Gong's analysis of 1.8 million new business deals closed in 2024 found that deals which closed had twice as many buyer contacts as deals which did not, that 77% of deals were multi-threaded, and that in deals over $50K multi-threading lifted win rates by an average of 130% ([Gong Labs, April 2025, updated May 2026](https://www.gong.io/resources/labs/data-shows-top-reps-dont-just-sell-they-orchestrate-with-ai/)). An earlier Gong study of more than 10 million sales conversations and 500,000 emails found winning deals averaged eight buyer contacts over email against three for losing deals, and had at least three buyer-side people in meetings across the cycle ([Gong, 2019, updated March 2026](https://www.gong.io/blog/army-of-champions-is-must-now-theres-data-to-prove-it)).

Take a hypothetical example. Vollan Systems, a 120-person logistics software company, is evaluating an outbound tool. Your contact, Teodor, runs the three-person sales team and loves the demo. He reports to a COO who owns the budget, shares a HubSpot instance with a RevOps lead who will have to connect it, and sits two desks from a founder who dislikes anything that touches company LinkedIn accounts. If Teodor is your only thread, three people who can stop the deal have never heard your answer to their question. If the COO, the RevOps lead and Teodor have each had one conversation with you, Teodor going on paternity leave in week six is an inconvenience, not a loss.

## How does multithreading in sales work in practice?

Multithreading is three repeated moves, not a personality trait. The sequence matters: find, enter, maintain.

1. **Find the other people who have a stake.** Before or during the first conversation, work out who else the decision touches: the budget owner, the person who has to set the tool up, the people who will use it, and anyone who can veto on security or legal grounds. The lesson on [what a buying committee is](/learn/what-is-a-buying-committee-in-b2b-sales) lists the roles and the question each one is quietly asking.
2. **Enter through the champion, not around them.** The person who brought you in should make the introductions, or at least know they are happening. Going straight to their boss is the fastest way to turn a champion into a blocker. The exact sentence that works is in the "this week" section below.
3. **Keep each thread alive separately.** A thread that had one call in March and nothing since is not a thread. After each meeting, every stakeholder gets something relevant to their own question: the RevOps lead gets the integration note, the COO gets the one-page business case, the users get the two-minute walkthrough.

Gartner's survey of 632 B2B buyers found buying groups of five to 16 people across as many as four functions, 74% of those groups showing unhealthy conflict during the decision, and groups that reached consensus 2.5 times more likely to call the deal high-quality ([Gartner, May 2025](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2025-05-07-gartner-sales-survey-finds-74-percent-of-b2b-buyer-teams-demonstrate-unhealthy-conflict-during-the-decision-process)). Multithreading is how a seller gets into that conflict early enough to help settle it.

## How many threads does a deal need?

Enough that no single departure kills it, and no more than you can keep warm. Every page on this topic assumes an enterprise deal. If you sell to companies of 1 to 200 people, the honest rule scales with the buyer's size, because the roles collapse into fewer people as the company gets smaller.

| Buyer's company size | Threads to aim for | Who they usually are | When one thread is fine |
|---|---|---|---|
| Under 20 people | 1 to 2 | The founder, and whoever will use the tool daily | Almost always: the founder is champion, budget and signature in one chair |
| 20 to 200 people | 2 to 3 | The person who felt the problem, their manager who owns the budget, the person who runs the systems | A purchase under the manager's own approval limit, bought by the person who will use it |
| 200 to 2,000 people | 3 to 5 | Champion, economic buyer, technical or security reviewer, one end user, procurement if the contract is large | Rarely: a renewal or a small add-on to an existing contract |
| Over 2,000 people | 5 or more | The above, plus a second executive and whoever ran the last comparable purchase | Never, if the deal matters to your quarter |

The small-company row is the one most pages get wrong. When we ran our own LinkedIn signals and AI SDR for two weeks and booked 14 qualified demos, the people who replied were founders complaining about cold outreach burnout. The champion and the economic buyer were the same person, so a second thread would have meant a conversation with a colleague who had no say. At that size, multithreading means knowing the user, not collecting contacts.

Single-threading is also fine when the deal is small relative to your contact's authority, when you are selling a monthly subscription the buyer can cancel, or when the sales cycle is under two weeks. The cost of a thread is time, yours and theirs, and a thread nobody needed is a wasted meeting on both sides.

## Multithreading vs single-threading vs multichannel: what is the difference?

Four terms get mixed together, and the mix-up leads people to do the wrong one. **Single-threading** is one seller to one buyer-side contact. **Multichannel outreach** means reaching the same person through several channels, such as LinkedIn, email and phone, and says nothing about how many people you reach. **Team selling** puts several people on your side of the deal, such as a rep plus a solutions engineer plus an executive sponsor. **Account-based prospecting** chooses and researches whole companies before any conversation, which is where multithreading usually starts.

| Term | How many people on the buyer's side | How many on your side | The question it answers |
|---|---|---|---|
| Single-threading | One | One | "Who is my contact here?" |
| Multithreading | Several, each with their own stake | One or more | "If my contact disappears, who else wants this?" |
| Multichannel outreach | One at a time | One | "Which channel will this person answer on?" |
| Team selling | Any | Several, matched to buyer roles | "Who on my side should talk to their CFO?" |
| Account-based prospecting | The whole buying group, mapped before contact | One or a pod | "Which companies, and who inside them, before I write a word?" |

Two things follow. You can be multichannel and single-threaded at the same time, which is the most common failure: five touches a week to one person, who then leaves. And multithreading does not require team selling; a founder selling alone can run three threads, as long as each person hears something meant for them. The lesson on [what account-based prospecting is](/learn/what-is-account-based-prospecting) covers the mapping step that comes before the first thread.

## How to use this this week

Three actions, each under an hour.

1. **Count the threads on every open deal.** For each opportunity past a first call, write down every buyer-side person who has replied to you in the last 30 days. One name against a 200-person company is a risk; log it as one and give that deal a single task: add one more person before the next meeting.
2. **Ask your champion the sentence that adds a thread without bypassing them.** Not "can I speak to your boss", which asks them to give up control. Instead: "To make this easy to approve, who else should I bring up to speed, and would it help if I drafted the note you send them?" You offer work, they keep ownership, and you get the name and usually the introduction. If they say no one, ask who sets up tools like this today; that person is your second thread.
3. **Find the second person before the first call on your next target account.** On LinkedIn, two people from the same company commenting on the same competitor's post, or one posting about the problem while a colleague follows your category's hashtag, is a buying group showing itself in public. Doing this by hand means a daily search and a spreadsheet; Intentsly watches LinkedIn keyword posts and the people engaging with competitor and industry posts and scores each against your ideal customer profile, so when two names from one company appear, you see the second thread before you have written to the first ([LinkedIn signals](/features/linkedin-signals) are the only source it monitors, so org charts and job boards stay a manual check). The [guide to finding decision makers on LinkedIn](/guides/find-decision-makers-on-linkedin) covers mapping from that first name to the budget owner.

## FAQ

### What does multithreading mean in sales, in simple terms?

It means having more than one relationship inside the company you are selling to. Instead of one contact who carries the deal, you talk to the budget owner, the person who will set the product up and the people who will use it, each about their own concern. If one of them leaves or goes quiet, the others keep the decision moving and you still know what is happening inside the account.

### What is the difference between multithreading and single-threading in sales?

Single-threading is one seller working with one buyer-side contact for the whole deal. Multithreading is the same seller working with several contacts at once. The difference shows when something changes: a single-threaded deal stalls the moment its one contact changes jobs or loses interest, while a multi-threaded deal has other people who still want the outcome and can carry it to the signature.

### What is an example of multi-threaded selling?

A seller is introduced by a sales manager at a 120-person company. Within two weeks she has also had one call with the COO who owns the budget, sent the RevOps lead a note on how the integration works, and given two reps a short walkthrough. When the sales manager goes on leave in week six, the COO asks RevOps for a recommendation, RevOps already has the answer, and the contract is signed on schedule.

### How many stakeholders should you engage in a deal?

Enough that no single person's departure kills it. At a company under 20 people, one or two, because the founder is usually champion, budget and signature in one chair. At 20 to 200 people, two or three: the person with the problem, their budget-owning manager and whoever runs the systems. Above 200, three to five, including a technical or security reviewer. Beyond that, five or more and a second executive.

### How do you multithread without going over your champion's head?

Make the champion the one who opens the doors. Ask who else should be brought up to speed to make approval easy, and offer to draft the note they send. Share with the champion anything you send to their colleagues, and keep them in every meeting you can. The champion keeps control of the process and gets credit for running it well, while you get the introductions you need.

### Is multithreading the same as multichannel outreach?

No. Multichannel means contacting the same person on several channels, such as LinkedIn, email and phone. Multithreading means contacting several people inside the same company. The two are often confused because both involve more messages. You can be multichannel and single-threaded at once, which is common and risky: many touches to one person who then leaves. Multithreading fixes the number of people, not the number of channels.
