> [!SUMMARY]
> - Find events by searching the problem your buyers have, not your own category, and filter to Events.
> - Click Attend: the attendee list only opens to people who have joined, and it shows at most about 1,000 names.
> - Filter the list to your ideal customer, add the people commenting in the event feed, and score a shortlist of 20 to 40.
> - Message attendees before the event, connect with the ones who answered after it, and follow up within 48 hours.

Here is how to find leads from LinkedIn events: search LinkedIn for the problem your buyers want solved and filter to Events, click Attend on two or three events whose registrants match your ideal customer, filter the attendee list by title, company and location, add the people commenting in the event feed, score a shortlist of 20 to 40, and message them with the event as the reason. Budget about two hours per event, split across the week before and the two days after.

## What you need to find leads from LinkedIn events

- A LinkedIn account that is warm enough to send 10 to 15 connection requests a day without a warning. A new or dormant account should run the [four-week warm-up routine](/guides/warm-up-linkedin-account-for-outreach) first.
- A one-line ideal customer profile with a title list, a company-size range and a region, because every filter in Step 3 comes from it. The lesson on [what an ideal customer profile is](/learn/what-is-an-ideal-customer-profile) gets you one in ten minutes.
- A spreadsheet with seven columns: name, title, company, event, hook, score, status. The scoring sheet in Step 5 fills it.
- Sales Navigator is optional. Everything below works on a free account; Navigator adds filters on the people once you have their names.
- Two hours per event: 30 minutes to pick and join, 45 minutes to filter and score, 45 minutes to write and send.

A LinkedIn event, for this guide, is any event page on LinkedIn with an attendee list: a webinar, a LinkedIn Live stream, an online conference or a meetup. Registering for one is a soft trigger event, a moment when someone shows they care about a topic; the lesson on [what a trigger event in sales is](/learn/what-is-a-trigger-event-in-sales) explains why that moment is worth more than a cold list.

## Step 1: Pick events where your buyers register, not your peers

The event decides the list, so spend the first half hour choosing. Most people search their own category ("sales automation webinar") and land in events full of other vendors. Search the buyer's problem instead.

1. **Search the problem.** In the LinkedIn search bar type the phrase a buyer would use, such as `hiring first sales rep` or `reduce churn SaaS`, press Enter and choose the Events filter at the top of the results. Try three or four phrasings; the Events filter returns both upcoming and past events.
2. **Check the host.** A software vendor hosting a demo-style webinar draws its own prospects, which may be yours too. An association, a media brand or a consultant hosting a how-to session draws practitioners. A tool vendor in your own category draws your competitors.
3. **Read the description for the audience line.** Hosts usually name who the event is for ("for founders raising a seed round", "for HR leads at 50 to 500 people"). If that line matches your ideal customer, the list probably will too.
4. **Check the size.** Under about 100 registrants gives you a handful of names; several thousand gives you a list you cannot see in full (Step 2 explains the ceiling). The ColdIQ tutorial opened for this guide suggests events with at least 100 people, and the filters in Step 3 typically remove most of the room ([ColdIQ, updated October 2026](https://coldiq.com/blog/extract-people-linkedin-event-phantombuster)).
5. **Prefer recurring events.** A monthly series from the same host gives you a fresh list every month and a hook that stays current.

| Test | Buyer event | Peer event |
|---|---|---|
| Search phrase that found it | The buyer's problem | Your product category |
| Host | Association, media brand, consultant, adjacent vendor | A vendor in your category |
| Audience line in description | Names your ideal customer's role | Names your own role |
| Speakers | Practitioners and operators | Other founders selling what you sell |
| Comments on the event page | Questions about the problem | Congratulations and "great lineup" |

Pick two or three events a week; more than that turns Step 3 into a scroll.

## Step 2: Click Attend so the attendee list opens

You will not see who is attending a LinkedIn event until you have joined it yourself. Every tool page opened for this guide states the same rule: PhantomBuster's own support article says LinkedIn only shows the attendee list once you have joined the event, Evaboot's tutorial says the full list is hidden until you confirm attendance, and ColdIQ's says the event's members are only displayed to people who joined ([PhantomBuster support, updated July 2026](https://support.phantombuster.com/hc/en-us/articles/26970982605714-How-to-Use-the-LinkedIn-Event-Guests-Export); [Evaboot](https://evaboot.com/blog/export-linkedin-event-attendees); [ColdIQ](https://coldiq.com/blog/extract-people-linkedin-event-phantombuster)). LinkedIn's own help pages on events could not be opened for this guide, so the rule is stated as those pages describe it.

1. **Open the event page and click Attend.** For a LinkedIn Live or a Page-hosted event, the button may read Register and ask for your email. Join under your own name; you are going to message people from this list and they will check.
2. **Open the attendee list.** On the event page, click the attendee count (it reads "N attendees" near the top) or open the Networking tab if the event has one. The list shows name, headline, location and connection degree.
3. **Note the ceiling.** PhantomBuster's support page says each event export is capped at the first 1,000 attendees because that is the maximum LinkedIn displays ([PhantomBuster support](https://support.phantombuster.com/hc/en-us/articles/26970982605714-How-to-Use-the-LinkedIn-Event-Guests-Export)). So a 4,000-person event shows you roughly the same number of names as a 1,000-person one, and the filters in Step 3 are what make a large event useful.
4. **Expect gaps.** Evaboot's FAQ notes that attendees can hide their attendance from first-degree connections who are not attending ([Evaboot](https://evaboot.com/blog/export-linkedin-event-attendees)), so the list is most of the room, not all of it.

## Step 3: Filter the attendee list to your ideal customer

The attendee list has its own search and filter controls, and they are enough for a shortlist. Work from your ideal customer profile and apply the filters in this order.

| Filter | Set it to | Why this order |
|---|---|---|
| Keyword (search box on the list) | One title word at a time: `founder`, `VP sales`, `head of people` | Headlines are free text; one word per pass catches variants |
| Connection degree | 2nd first, then 3rd | 2nd-degree profiles show shared connections you can mention |
| Location | Your region or time zone | Removes the names you could never serve |
| Current company | Your target accounts, if the list has the filter | Only present on some events; skip if absent |
| Industry | The buyer's industry, not yours | Removes the vendors who joined for the same reason you did |

Run the keyword passes one title at a time and copy each match into the spreadsheet with name, title, company and event. Do not copy the whole list. Linkyfy's guide and GTM Bud's both point out that LinkedIn's terms restrict copying member data with automated tools and that scrapers are a known cause of account restrictions ([Linkyfy, October 2026](https://www.linkyfy.ai/blog/find-leads-from-linkedin-events/); [GTM Bud, August 2026](https://gtmbud.com/blog/linkedin-events-for-lead-generation)). A hand-built list of 40 is also a better list, because every name on it has passed your eyes once.

If the event has no filter controls, use the keyword search alone and scroll; one pass over 300 names takes about ten minutes.

## Step 4: Add the people who engage in the event feed

The attendee list tells you who registered. The event feed tells you who cares: the people who comment or ask questions under the host's and speakers' posts have told you what they want to know.

1. **Open the event feed.** On the event page, scroll below the description to the posts. For a LinkedIn Live, the comments during the stream appear here too.
2. **Read every comment on the host's announcement posts.** Copy each commenter who fits your ideal customer into the spreadsheet, and paste their comment into the hook column word for word. That sentence becomes the first line of your message.
3. **Check who reacted to the speaker's posts about the event.** Click the reaction count under each post. Reactors are a weaker signal than commenters, so give them a lower score in Step 5.
4. **Repeat the day after the event**, when hosts post recordings and takeaways; the commenters there are the people who watched.

This step takes the most time by hand and is the one worth automating. Intentsly's [LinkedIn signals](/features/linkedin-signals) watch the engagement on the posts of the hosts and speakers you choose, so the people commenting about the event land in your list already scored against your profile, without you refreshing the feed.

> [!TIP]
> Hosts often post a "who's coming?" thread a few days before the event. The replies are a self-selected list of attendees who are happy to be seen, and a reply of your own ("Looking forward to the pricing section") puts your name in front of all of them at no cost.

## Step 5: Score the shortlist to 20 to 40 people

A 1,000-person list filtered to 150 is still too many to write to properly. Score each name on four questions and keep the top 20 to 40, which is what one person can message with a real first line in 45 minutes.

| Question | 2 points | 1 point | 0 points |
|---|---|---|---|
| Does the title match your buyer? | Exact title in your ideal customer profile | Adjacent title at the right level | Wrong function or level |
| Does the company fit? | Size, industry and region all match | Two of three match | One or none |
| Did they do more than register? | Commented or asked a question in the feed | Reacted to a speaker post | Registered only |
| Can you reach them? | 2nd-degree with a shared connection you know | 2nd-degree, no known shared connection | 3rd-degree or outside your region |

Keep everyone at 6 or above and record the score and the hook (their comment, the session they asked about, or the event title if nothing else). ## Step 6: Message attendees before the event, then connect after it

Timing and channel matter more here than wording: a short message from the list before the event, a connection request with the event as the hook after it.

1. **Before the event (three to seven days out): send a short message from the attendee list.** Some attendees show a Message button to other attendees on the list even without a connection. Expandi's H1 2026 report, built on 70,130+ campaigns, says that LinkedIn no longer lets you message an entire event audience and that only attendees showing a message button can be messaged this way ([Expandi, State of LinkedIn Outreach H1 2026](https://expandi.io/blog/state-of-li-outreach-h1-2026/)). GTM Bud's guide reports that these land as message requests rather than inbox messages ([GTM Bud](https://gtmbud.com/blog/linkedin-events-for-lead-generation)), so keep them to one question about the event.
2. **During the event: comment with a real question.** The people you message afterwards saw it.
3. **After the event (within 48 hours): send connection requests to the people who replied or engaged.** A connection request with a note that names the session and their comment is the main channel for everyone who did not show a Message button. Expandi's benchmark across 13.2 million requests from May 2025 to April 2026 puts the average acceptance at 28.5% and the average reply to a connection-request note at 3.0%, so the note's job is to be accepted, and the message that follows acceptance does the selling ([Expandi, LinkedIn Outreach Benchmarks 2026](https://expandi.io/blog/linkedin-outreach-benchmarks-2026/)).
4. **Stay inside the request limit.** Expandi's H1 2026 report describes LinkedIn capping connection requests at 80 to 100 a week for most users ([Expandi H1 2026](https://expandi.io/blog/state-of-li-outreach-h1-2026/)), and LinkedIn does not publish the figure. Two events a week at 20 to 40 requests each sits inside it; three events does not, which is why Step 1 says two or three.

The same Expandi report puts the reply rate of event campaigns, meaning messages to attendees of a shared event, at 14.21% against a 10.3% average for LinkedIn messages overall ([Expandi H1 2026](https://expandi.io/blog/state-of-li-outreach-h1-2026/)). That is one vendor's customers, not a LinkedIn figure, but it is the only published number for this method, and it points the same way as the logic: a shared event is a reason to write that a cold list never has.

Three templates, one per moment, each under 40 words with one question. The guide on [how to write a LinkedIn outreach message that gets a reply](/guides/write-linkedin-outreach-message) explains why the question matters more than the pitch.

```message
Saw you're registered for Thursday's session on hiring a first sales rep. We're deciding whether to hire or outsource this quarter. Which way are you leaning?
```

```message
Your question in the comments about ramp time was the one I wanted answered too. I've got a note from the speaker's reply. Want me to send it over?
```

```message
We were both at the churn session yesterday. The bit on exit interviews changed how we're running ours. How are you handling them on your side?
```

## Step 7: Follow up within 48 hours and log what happened

The event hook decays fast. GTM Bud's guide puts the useful window at about two weeks and recommends following up within 48 hours of the event ([GTM Bud, August 2026](https://gtmbud.com/blog/linkedin-events-for-lead-generation)); a simpler rule is that if you have not sent the follow-up by the second morning after the event, drop the event reference and write a fresh message.

1. **Send one follow-up to everyone who accepted, within 48 hours.** Name the session and ask one question about their situation. No deck.
2. **Send one more to the people who replied before the event and went quiet**, referencing the recording or a takeaway the host posted.
3. **Mark the status column.** Sent, accepted, replied, meeting. Anyone silent after two follow-ups goes to "parked" and gets the next event's hook instead.
4. **Log the event's totals**: names filtered, shortlisted, messaged, accepted, replied. After three events you know which hosts produce replies.

## Worked example: Harbor Analytics (hypothetical)

Harbor Analytics is a made-up 12-person consultancy that builds revenue dashboards for B2B SaaS companies with 20 to 200 staff. Its founder runs the routine on Tuesday morning and Friday afternoon. The numbers are illustrative, not measured.

Step 1: she searches `SaaS metrics board reporting`, filters to Events, and finds a monthly "Metrics that matter to your board" webinar hosted by a SaaS community, 1,800 registrants, audience line "for founders and finance leads at seed to Series B companies". A second result, a dashboard vendor's product demo, fails the host test and is skipped.

Step 2: she clicks Attend. The list shows about 1,000 names.

Step 3: keyword passes for `founder`, `CEO`, `CFO`, `head of finance` and `revops`, second-degree first, Europe only. 140 names go into the sheet.

Step 4: the host's "who's coming?" post has 60 comments. Eleven commenters fit the profile and were not already in the sheet; their comments go into the hook column. One asks whether net revenue retention belongs on a board deck at all.

Step 5: scoring leaves 34 people at 6 or above, nine of them with a comment as the hook.

Step 6: on Tuesday she sends 14 short messages to the attendees who show a Message button, asking which metric they struggle to report. Four reply before the event. During the webinar she asks one question in the comments. On Friday she sends 28 connection requests naming the session; the nine with a comment hook get the comment quoted back.

Step 7, the following Tuesday: she sends the follow-up to everyone who accepted and logs outcomes. Total time across the week: about two and a half hours.

## Where this goes wrong

**Joining events in your own category.** The list is full of competitors, the filters leave a dozen names, and the hook ("we were both at the sales automation webinar") tells the reader you sell sales automation. Search the buyer's problem instead, as in Step 1.

**Copying the whole list and messaging it.** Beyond the terms-of-service risk described in Step 3, an event list messaged in bulk reads as exactly that, because 1,000 people got the same "we were both at the event" opener on the same day. Evaboot's own tutorial notes that you cannot bulk-message an event's attendees inside LinkedIn ([Evaboot](https://evaboot.com/blog/export-linkedin-event-attendees)), and the tools that work around that are the ones that get accounts restricted. Twenty names with a real first line beat a thousand without one.

**Treating registration as intent.** Many people on a webinar list registered for the recording and never think about the topic again. The scoring sheet in Step 5 weights the people who commented or asked a question, and the event feed in Step 4 is where you find them. If you only have time for one of Steps 3 and 4, do Step 4.

## What to do with the list

An event gives you 20 to 40 people with a reason to hear from you this week, and the reason expires. Two habits keep it a pipeline rather than a one-off.

Run the routine on a calendar, two events a week, and log the totals from Step 7 so that after a month you know which hosts and which search phrases produce replies. Treat the event as the first signal, not the only one: the people who accepted but did not reply will post, change jobs or comment on a competitor within a quarter, and that is the message that lands. ## FAQ

### Can you see who is attending a LinkedIn event?

Yes, once you have joined it. The tool pages opened for this guide agree that LinkedIn hides the attendee list from people who have not clicked Attend, and PhantomBuster's support page says the visible list stops at about 1,000 names. Some attendees also hide their attendance from non-attending connections, so the list is most of the room rather than all of it.

### Do I need to attend the event to see the attendees?

You need to register, which means clicking Attend or Register on the event page. You do not need to watch it, although your messages are stronger if you did and can name a specific moment. Joining puts your name on the same list for everyone else, so keep your profile honest and current before you start.

### Can you message LinkedIn event attendees without connecting?

Sometimes. Some attendees show a Message button to other attendees on the list, and Expandi's H1 2026 report says LinkedIn no longer allows messaging the entire audience, only those with the button. Expect these messages to land as message requests. For everyone else, a connection request with a note naming the event is the channel.

### Can you export a LinkedIn event attendee list?

Not natively as an attendee. Organisers can export their own registrants from the event's analytics, as [Contrast's guide](https://www.getcontrast.io/mini-guides/export-linkedin-event-attendees) describes. Third-party tools export the list for attendees, but their own pages say the export stops at about 1,000 names, and both Linkyfy and GTM Bud warn that automated copying of member data breaches LinkedIn's terms and is a known cause of restrictions.

### Should I message attendees before or after the event?

Both, with different channels. Before the event, use the Message button on the list where it appears and ask one question about the session, so your name is familiar. After the event, within 48 hours, send connection requests to the people who replied or engaged, naming the session and their comment. The hook fades within about two weeks, so do not save it for later.

### How many people should I contact from one event?

Twenty to forty, scored as in Step 5. That is what one person can message with a real first line in 45 minutes, and two events a week at that size stays inside the 80 to 100 weekly connection requests that Expandi's H1 2026 report describes LinkedIn applying to most accounts. More names than that and the messages start to read as a mail merge.
