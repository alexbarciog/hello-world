Here is how to warm up a LinkedIn account for outreach: spend week one finishing the profile and reading, not sending; spend week two engaging with the exact people you plan to contact later; send five connection requests a day from week two and raise the number only while at least three in ten are accepted and LinkedIn shows no warning; reach your working pace in week four. A new account takes four weeks and about 20 minutes a day. The calendar, the hold rule and the routine follow.

## What you need to warm up a LinkedIn account for outreach

You need five things before day one. None of them costs money.

| What | Why it matters during warm-up | Where to get it |
|---|---|---|
| A finished profile: photo, headline that names who you help, About section, current role with a description | The first 50 people you ask to connect will open the profile before they accept. An empty one is declined, and declines are the number you are trying to keep down. | Step 1 below; the free [LinkedIn Profile Analyzer](/linkedin-profile-analyzer) scores it |
| A target list of 50 to 100 people who fit your ideal customer, plus 5 to 10 competitor or industry accounts they follow | The warm-up actions have to go somewhere. Pointing them at your future buyers turns four weeks of "engagement" into a list you can message in week three. | [How to find decision makers on LinkedIn](/guides/find-decision-makers-on-linkedin) |
| One device, one browser, one home or office connection | Two of the ranking guides warn that logins from new devices, shared VPNs or data-centre IP addresses during the first month trigger verification prompts. Keep the account boring. | Your own laptop, not a shared tool login |
| A log: a spreadsheet with one row per day | The hold-or-advance rule in Step 4 needs two counts you cannot get from LinkedIn in one place: requests sent and requests accepted. | Any sheet; columns in Step 4 |
| 20 minutes a day on working days, four weeks, no automation until week four | Every page that ranks for this query, including the automation vendors, keeps at least the first week manual, and most keep the first three. | Your calendar |

## Step 1: Finish the profile before anyone sees it (days 1 to 3)

Do this before a single request goes out. Every request you send is also a profile view in the other direction, and acceptance is the metric that decides how fast you can move.

Work through the fields in this order, because this is the order a stranger reads them:

1. **Photo.** A recent head-and-shoulders shot, face taking up most of the frame, plain background. No logo, no group photo.
2. **Headline.** Who you help and with what, in plain words: "Payroll compliance for UK staffing agencies" beats "Founder | Visionary | Helping businesses grow". The headline shows in the connection request itself, so it does more work than anything else on the page.
3. **About.** Three short paragraphs: the problem you work on, how you work on it, and one sentence on who should message you. Write it in the first person.
4. **Current role.** Company, dates, and two lines on what the company does. Add the company page link so the logo appears.
5. **Two or more past roles, education, and a location.** These are the fields that make a profile read as a person rather than a shell.
6. **Custom URL.** Settings, Edit public profile and URL, set it to your name.

Run the result through the [LinkedIn Profile Analyzer](/linkedin-profile-analyzer) and fix what it flags. Then leave the profile alone for the rest of the month. Rewriting the headline every week during warm-up is itself a change pattern you do not need.

Do not post in week one. Two of the ranking pages ask for two or three posts in the first seven days; a new account with no connections posting to an empty feed does nothing for you, and a post is a public commitment you cannot withdraw. Post from week two, once there is someone to read it.

## Step 2: Spend week one reading, following and building the engagement list

Week one is reading week. Nothing you do in it sends a notification to a stranger except a follow and a comment, and both are welcome.

Each day, for 20 minutes:

1. **Follow 5 to 8 accounts from the target list and 1 or 2 competitor or industry pages.** Following is not connecting; it needs no acceptance and it fills your feed with the people you will later message.
2. **Save three searches.** In the LinkedIn search bar, search a phrase your buyers use when they are frustrated, switch to Posts, sort by Latest, and bookmark the results URL. Repeat for a competitor's name and for your category. Open the three bookmarks daily; they are your signal feed for the month.
3. **React to 3 to 5 posts from people on the list.** Not viral posts from strangers. Reactions on your future buyers' posts put your name in their notifications before you ever ask for anything.
4. **Leave 1 or 2 real comments.** Two sentences that add something: a question about a detail in the post, or a related thing you have seen. "Great post!" is the kind of one-line comment the ranking guides single out as reading as automated.
5. **Log the day.** Who you engaged with and what their post was about. This is the start of your signal log.

Search strings that work for this, with the Posts filter on and sorted by Latest:

```
"anyone recommend" <your category>
"switching from" <competitor name>
"frustrated with" <the tool or process you replace>
<competitor name> pricing
```

The people who comment on a competitor's posts are the warmest audience you will find on LinkedIn, and reacting to their comments during warm-up means they recognise your name in week three. [How to track competitor LinkedIn post engagers](/blog/track-competitor-linkedin-post-engagers) shows three ways to collect those names by hand.

## Step 3: Send the first connection requests to people who already know you (week 2)

From day 8, start connecting. The first requests go to people who will certainly accept: colleagues, former colleagues, customers, suppliers, anyone whose comment you replied to in week one. Five a day on working days, spread across the day, never a burst of five in one minute. Weekends are for reading.

The point of starting with warm names is the acceptance count. Your first 25 requests set the pattern LinkedIn sees, and a run of accepted requests is the pattern you want on record before you start asking strangers.

Add a note to each request in week two. The note is one or two sentences and names something specific:

```
Hi <first name>, I have been following your posts on <topic> since the one about <detail>.
Would be good to connect.
```

```
Hi <first name>, we both commented on <poster>'s post about <topic> last week.
Connecting so I can keep following your take on it.
```

No link, no pitch, no mention of your product. The request is the handshake; the conversation comes after acceptance, in Step 5.

Withdraw anything still pending after two weeks. Go to My Network, Manage, Sent, and withdraw the oldest. The ranking guides differ on how long to leave them, from one week to three, and none points to a published rule, so the safe habit is a clean Sent tab.

Post your first update at the end of week two: one observation from the posts you read, no link. You now have a dozen or two connections who will see it.

## Step 4: Raise the volume on a hold rule, not a calendar date (weeks 3 and 4)

The calendar below is the shape every ranking guide agrees on, with the daily numbers in the band where they overlap. What makes it work is the last column: you move to the next row only when the previous week's counts say so.

| Week | Connection requests a day | Messages a day (accepted connections only) | Reactions and comments a day | Advance to the next week only if |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0 | 0 | 3 to 5 reactions, 1 to 2 comments | Profile finished, 30 or more follows, three saved searches |
| 2 | 5, warm names first | 0 to 2, replies only | 5 to 8 reactions, 2 to 3 comments | At least 3 of every 10 requests accepted, no warning shown |
| 3 | 10, target list | 5, to new connections | 8 to 10 reactions, 3 comments | At least 3 of every 10 accepted, no warning, no verification prompt |
| 4 | 15 | 10 to 15 | 10 reactions, 3 to 5 comments | Same rule; hold here for two more weeks before any further increase |

Keep the log in this shape, one row per day:

```
date | requests sent | requests accepted (as of today) | messages sent | replies | reactions | comments | warnings (none / too many invitations / verification / other)
```

The hold rule in words: count the requests you sent in the last seven days and how many of them are now accepted. Three in ten or better, and no warning in the log, means you may move up one row next week. Fewer than three in ten, or any warning, means you stay on the current row. A restriction of any kind means you go back to the row for week two once it lifts.

Why 15 a day in week four and not the 25 to 30 some guides reach by then? Because 15 is the number Intentsly recommends for every LinkedIn account connected to it. Intentsly caps each account at 30 messages and 30 connection requests a day, allows up to 2 accounts per plan, and recommends 15, because the account has to survive the quarter, not just the campaign. If you would rather not count requests, acceptances and replies by hand, the [AI SDR](/features/ai-sdr) sends the connection requests and messages for you inside those same limits and holds the conversation until the lead books a call.

For context on the ceiling you are staying under: the limits pages opened today describe a weekly invitation cap of roughly 100 that LinkedIn enforces but does not publish, the same across free and paid plans ([Overloop, July 2026](https://overloop.com/blog/linkedin-limits)), and suggest new accounts stay at 10 to 15 requests a day and 40 to 60 a week in their first month ([PhantomBuster, updated July 2026](https://phantombuster.com/blog/linkedin-automation/linkedin-automation-safe-limits-2026/)). Both figures are community-reported, which is why this guide works from counts you can see in your own log rather than a limit nobody can confirm. Week four at 15 a day on five working days is 75 a week, under that ceiling with room for the occasional extra; the same 15 on seven days would be 105, which is why the calendar rests at weekends.

## Step 5: Message accepted connections only, and name the signal

Messages start in week three and go only to people who accepted a request from you. Messaging strangers is what InMail is for, and it is not part of a warm-up.

The first message names the reason you connected, asks one question, and stops. No link, no deck, no calendar invite:

```
Thanks for connecting, <first name>. Your comment on <poster>'s post about <topic> stuck with me,
especially <detail>. Is that still the main thing getting in the way, or has it moved on?
```

If the person replied to a comment of yours in week one or two, open with that instead. The warm-up gave you the material; use it. [How to write a LinkedIn outreach message that gets a reply](/guides/write-linkedin-outreach-message) covers the second and third messages and what to do with a "not now".

Keep messages to the daily count in the table. The messages column matters as much as the requests column: a brand-new account sending 30 near-identical messages a day in week three is the pattern the vendor guides warn about most.

## Step 6: Watch the four signs that you are moving too fast

Check the log and the account every day. Four things mean stop.

| Sign | What it usually means | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| "You're sending too many invitations" or an invitation limit message when you try to connect | You have hit the weekly ceiling or a short-term rate check | Stop sending requests for seven days. Keep reacting and commenting. Resume at the previous week's row. |
| A request to verify your identity, or a forced logout followed by a security check | The login or activity pattern looked automated or came from a new place | Complete the verification from your usual device. Send nothing for 48 hours. Check nothing else is logged into the account. |
| Acceptances fall below three in ten for a week | Your targeting drifted, your note stopped matching, or the profile no longer fits who you ask | Hold volume. Re-read the last 20 profiles you asked. Tighten the list before you widen the sending. |
| Messages stop getting replies while acceptances hold | The message is the problem, not the account | Hold the message count. Rewrite the opener around the signal you saw, not around you. |

A restriction is not the end of the account. The guides opened for this page that cover recovery agree on the shape: pause everything, complete whatever verification LinkedIn asks for, wait for the restriction to lift, and restart well below where you stopped. [PhantomBuster's limits page](https://phantombuster.com/blog/linkedin-automation/linkedin-automation-safe-limits-2026/) says to restart on week-one settings, and its [warm-up calendar](https://phantombuster.com/blog/?p=11428) warns that a second flag soon after the first means a longer restriction. This guide restarts at the week-two row because week one has no requests to restart; the point is the same, slower on purpose.

## A worked example: Halden Compliance's first four weeks (hypothetical)

Halden Compliance is a hypothetical two-person consultancy that sells payroll compliance reviews to UK staffing agencies. The founder, Priya, creates a new LinkedIn account on a Monday because her old one is personal and has 90 connections from a different career. The numbers below are illustrative, not measured.

**Week 1.** Priya finishes the profile on Monday and Tuesday, headline "Payroll compliance reviews for UK staffing agencies". She builds a list of 80 staffing-agency owners and finance leads and follows 6 a day on working days, plus two payroll software company pages and one industry body. Her three saved searches are "umbrella company" compliance, a competitor's name, and "payroll audit" staffing. She reacts to 4 posts a day and leaves one comment, usually a question about a detail. By Friday she has 30 follows, 5 comments and a log with 11 names of agency owners who commented on the competitor's posts.

**Week 2.** She sends 5 requests a day: former colleagues on Monday and Tuesday, two clients and three agency owners on Wednesday, then the rest of the 11 agency owners whose comments she had replied to. By Friday, 25 sent and 16 accepted. No warnings. She posts once, a short observation on a question that came up three times in the saved searches. The rule says advance.

**Week 3.** 10 requests a day to the target list, each with a note that names the post or comment she saw. 5 messages a day to the people who accepted in week two, each opening with the thing they wrote. By Friday: 50 sent that week, 17 accepted so far, 3 in 10 and holding. Replies to 8 of 25 messages, two of which are "send me what you do". No warnings. Advance.

**Week 4.** 15 requests a day, 10 to 12 messages a day. On Wednesday she withdraws 9 requests pending from weeks two and three. On Thursday LinkedIn asks her to verify her identity after she logs in from a client's office; she completes it on her own laptop that evening and sends nothing on Friday. The log shows the gap. By the end of week four Halden has about 55 connections who are almost all in the target market, a signal log with 60 rows, and one review call booked. She holds at 15 a day for the next fortnight, as the rule says, before considering a second account.

## Where this goes wrong

Three mistakes come up again and again across the guides opened for this page and the warning signs in Step 6.

**Treating the calendar as permission.** Week three says 10 a day, so 10 a day go out whatever the acceptances say. The calendar is the ceiling; the hold rule is the permission. An account where fewer than three in ten accept is telling you the list or the note is wrong, and sending more only records more declines.

**Warming up on the wrong people.** Forty likes on viral posts from strangers age the account and build nothing. If the reactions, comments and follows in weeks one and two do not go to your target list and the posts your buyers engage with, week three starts from zero: no recognition, no signal, no reason in the note. The warm-up is the research phase of your outbound; spend it on the people you will message.

**Letting anything else touch the account.** A VA logging in from another country, a browser extension that scrapes the feed, a second tool "just for profile views", or your own login from a hotel Wi-Fi on day nine. [The Lead Lab](https://theleadlab.com/blog/linkedin-account-warmup/) and [Closely](https://blog.closelyhq.com/the-complete-guide-to-linkedin-warm-up-strategies-for-new-accounts/) both list device, location and shared-session changes among the causes of verification prompts, and nothing in the other guides contradicts them. One device, one place, one tool at a time, and none of them until week four.

## What to do with the list

By the end of week four you have something the ranking guides never mention as an outcome: 50 to 100 connections who fit your ideal customer, a log of what each one posted or commented on, and a handful of replies. That is a signal-based outbound list, and the account that holds it is now warm enough to run it.

Sort the log by signal, not by date. Everyone who commented on a competitor's post is one segment; everyone who asked a question your saved search caught is another; everyone who accepted but never replied is a third. Each segment gets its own opener, written around the signal. [How to set up signal-based outbound](/guides/set-up-signal-based-outbound) is the seven-step version of running that list every week with the same 30-a-day caps, and the [guides index](/guides) has the pieces around it: finding decision makers, qualifying before you message, and following up.

Hold week-four volume for at least two weeks before raising it, and only raise it while the hold rule still says yes. ## FAQ

### How long does it take to warm up a LinkedIn account for outreach?

Four weeks for a new account, following the calendar above, with a further two weeks holding at week-four volume before any increase. A dormant account that has not been used for months needs about two weeks: one week of reading and engaging, then requests from the week-two row. An active account you are moving from personal to outbound use needs no warm-up, only the hold rule.

### How many connection requests can a new LinkedIn account send per day?

Start at five a day in week two, ten in week three and fifteen in week four, and move up only when at least three in ten of the previous week's requests were accepted with no warning. The limits pages opened for this guide describe a weekly ceiling of roughly 100 that LinkedIn enforces but does not publish ([Overloop](https://overloop.com/blog/linkedin-limits)); week four at fifteen a day stays well inside it.

### Do I need to warm up an old LinkedIn account that has been dormant?

Yes, but for a shorter time. An account with a history behaves better than a new one, so one week of following, reacting and commenting is usually enough before requests start at the week-two row. Finish the profile first, since the headline and About may be years out of date, and withdraw any pending requests left from the account's previous life before sending new ones.

### Does LinkedIn Premium or Sales Navigator raise the connection request limit?

Not according to the limits pages opened for this guide, which describe the weekly invitation ceiling as the same across free and paid plans ([Overloop](https://overloop.com/blog/linkedin-limits), [PhantomBuster](https://phantombuster.com/blog/linkedin-automation/linkedin-automation-safe-limits-2026/)). Paid plans add InMail credits and more search results, which help you choose who to ask, not how many you can ask. Warm a paid account on the same calendar.

### Can I use automation while warming up a LinkedIn account?

Not for the first three weeks. The automation vendors ranking for this query keep at least the first week manual in their own guides, and most keep the first three, because a new account acting at machine pace is what triggers verification. From week four, run any tool at or below the table's numbers; Intentsly, for example, caps each account at 30 requests and 30 messages a day and recommends 15.

### What should I do if LinkedIn restricts my account during the warm-up?

Stop every action, including reactions. Complete whatever verification LinkedIn asks for from the device you normally use. Make sure no tool, extension or other person is logged into the account. When the restriction lifts, restart at the week-two row rather than where you stopped, and hold there for a full week before moving up. Record the restriction in the log so you can see what preceded it.
