X (Twitter) buying signals for B2B are public posts in which someone asks for a tool recommendation, complains about a vendor by name, compares two products, announces a switch, or describes a problem your product fixes. You find them with X's search box, not a paid tool: paste one of the eight search strings below, check the poster's bio, and reply in public before you ever send a DM. The whole routine takes about 15 minutes a day. The searches, the triage and the replies follow.

## Is X worth watching for B2B buying signals at all?

Yes if your buyers are founders, developers, marketers, salespeople or people in fintech and crypto, because those groups complain about tools on X in public and by name. No if you sell to procurement-led buyers in manufacturing, healthcare, legal or the public sector, who rarely post about vendors and whose accounts are mostly silent. Before you build a routine, run the test in the next section once. If it returns fewer than five usable posts for your category in the last 30 days, X is not your channel and the hour is better spent on LinkedIn.

A second reason to be careful: X's own search tooling is thinner than the vendor guides suggest. X's help page says [advanced search is available when you are logged in on X.com](https://help.x.com/en/using-x/x-advanced-search), with fields for all words, exact phrases, any of several words, excluded words, hashtags, language, posts from, to or mentioning an account, place and date range. A separate help page says [saved searches are currently not available on x.com](https://help.x.com/en/using-x/saving-searches) and work only in the app, from the Explore tab. So on desktop you will keep your searches in a text file and paste them, which is fine and is what the routine below assumes.

## What X (Twitter) buying signals for B2B look like

A buying signal is a public action that shows someone is closer to buying than the average account; the [definition and a test for separating signals from noise](/learn/what-is-a-buying-signal) are in the Learn section. On X the signal is almost always text, which is good news: text can be searched. The eight below are ranked by how clearly the post shows intent to buy and how openly it invites an answer, which together decide how often a reply turns into a conversation. Replace the bracketed words with your competitor, your category and your buyer's title.

| Rank | Signal | Paste into X search | Why it ranks here |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Asking for a recommendation | `"anyone recommend" OR "any recommendations" OR "what do you use for" "[category]" -filter:links` | The poster has budget and a timeline and invited answers |
| 2 | Naming a competitor and a complaint | `"[competitor]" ("frustrated" OR "annoying" OR "broken" OR "price increase" OR "cancelling")` | A named vendor plus a named pain is the clearest switch intent on the platform |
| 3 | Comparing two named tools | `"[competitor A]" "[competitor B]" (vs OR "or" OR "compared")` | A shortlist exists; you want to be the third name |
| 4 | Announcing a switch or a migration | `"switching from" OR "moved off" OR "migrating from" "[competitor]"` | The decision is made; the same account will evaluate neighbours in the stack |
| 5 | Describing the problem without naming a tool | `"[pain phrase]" ("how do you" OR "anyone solved" OR "tired of")` | Earlier stage, more noise, but no competitor is in the room yet |
| 6 | A hiring or team change | `"hiring" ("SDR" OR "head of sales" OR "first sales hire") from:[account]` | Headcount in your buyer's function means new process and new tools within a quarter |
| 7 | A question under a competitor's post | `to:[competitor handle] (pricing OR "does it" OR alternative)` | People ask competitors in public what they would never ask you in private |
| 8 | A launch, funding or milestone post | `"we just raised" OR "launching today" OR "hit $" "[category]"` | Budget arrives, but the post is not about your category, so conversion is lowest |

Two operators that make every row usable: `since:YYYY-MM-DD` limits results to recent posts, and `min_faves:5` (any number) removes the posts nobody saw. A third-party guide to X search, [Sendible's operator list](https://www.sendible.com/insights/how-to-use-twitter-advanced-search), covers `from:`, `to:`, `since:`, `until:`, `min_faves:`, `filter:links`, `near:` and exclusion with a leading minus, if you want to build your own strings. Treat the table as a starting set; the phrasing your buyers actually use will come from the first week of reading results.

The 24 worked [buying signal examples, each with a reply](/blog/buying-signals-examples), include several of these in their LinkedIn form if you want to see the same signal across channels.

## How do you find them without a tool?

Open x.com logged in, click into the search box and paste a string from the table, then choose "Latest" instead of "Top" so you see posts in time order. Read the first 30 results. Expect two kinds of noise: people selling the category rather than buying it, and old posts that surfaced because they were popular. Add `-filter:links` to remove most vendor posts and `since:` with a date seven days back to remove the old ones.

Rows 1 to 5 are the daily searches. Run rows 6 to 8 weekly, because they produce fewer posts and age slowly. Keep the strings in one text file with a date next to each so you remember which `since:` value you last used. If your category is noisy, split the search by language with `lang:en` and by excluding the words that keep appearing in irrelevant results.

Monitoring tools exist for this, and most of the pages ranking for this keyword are written by one. Their cost has a floor set by X itself: X's API is [priced per use, at $0.005 per post read and capped at 3 million post reads per monthly billing cycle](https://docs.x.com/x-api/getting-started/pricing), so a tool watching dozens of keywords across the whole platform pays for every post it looks at. That is why "free X monitoring" plans are thin or absent, and why, for one or two categories, the search box is the right tool. If you later want alerts delivered to you, the [comparison of keyword monitoring tools](/blog/reddit-keyword-monitoring-for-leads) covers which vendors read X as an add-on and what they charge, checked against their pricing pages.

## How do you cut the noise with a private List?

Open searches return students, vendors, journalists and bots next to buyers. A List fixes most of that, because a List timeline shows posts only from the accounts you put on it. X's help page says [the default setting for a List is public and the Private slider makes it accessible only to you](https://help.x.com/en/using-x/x-lists); names cannot exceed 25 characters or begin with a number. Keep prospect Lists private. Nobody should learn they are on your list by getting a notification.

Build three, each with 20 to 50 accounts. First, competitor customers: the authors of the posts that rows 2, 3 and 4 return over your first two weeks. Second, in-profile buyers: people whose bio states the title and company type you sell to, found by searching those titles and reading the bios by hand. Third, the loud accounts in your category: the ten or twenty people whose posts your buyers reply to, because recommendation asks and complaints pile up in those reply threads.

Once the Lists exist, read their timelines before you run the open searches. Fifteen minutes on three List timelines, newest first, usually turns up more usable posts than an hour of open searching, and you already know who the poster is, which shortens the triage below.

## How do you tell a signal from a sentence?

A matching post is a sentence. It becomes a lead only after a two-minute check that most guides skip. Open the poster's profile and answer four questions in order:

1. Is there a company? Look at the bio, the pinned post and the link. No company and no role means a hobbyist, a student or an anonymous account. Drop it.
2. Does the role buy or influence your category? A founder, a head of the relevant function or the person who clearly runs the stack counts. A junior account with a complaint is still useful as a door into the company, but it is not the buyer.
3. Is the company in your ideal customer profile? Check the size and the industry on the company's own site or LinkedIn page. A perfect post from a company you would never close is a time sink.
4. Is the post recent and still open? Anything older than two weeks has probably been answered or forgotten. Skip it unless the signal is row 2 or 4, where the pain persists.

Write the ones that pass into a sheet with five columns: date, X handle, LinkedIn URL, signal row, post link. That sheet is your pipeline for this channel and it tells you in a month whether X deserves the 15 minutes. If you already run signal-based outbound on other channels, the X sheet slots into the same process; the [seven-step setup for signal-based outbound](/guides/set-up-signal-based-outbound) shows where.

## What do you say, and where?

Reply in public first, on the post itself, and only then move to a private channel. Three reasons. First, a public reply is visible to everyone in the thread, including the other people with the same problem. Second, X's help page on Direct Messages says that [a person you do not follow can message you only if you opted in to messages from anyone, or from Verified users, or you messaged them first](https://help.x.com/en/using-x/direct-messages); otherwise your message lands in their Requests folder or cannot be sent at all, so the public reply is often the only door. Third, X's automation rules are strict: the policy says [you may not send unsolicited Direct Messages in a bulk or automated manner](https://help.x.com/en/rules-and-policies/x-automation), that sending automated replies to posts based on keyword searches alone is not permitted, and that you may not like posts in an automated manner. Any tool that promises auto-replies or auto-DMs from keyword matches is asking you to break those rules with your own account.

The public reply has one job: be the most useful answer in the thread. Hypothetical examples, to show the shape rather than to copy:

- Row 1 (recommendation request): "Depends on whether you need [feature A] or [feature B]. If A, [competitor] is solid. If B, we built [product] for exactly that; happy to show you how it handles [their stated case] if useful."
- Row 2 (complaint about a competitor): "Had the same issue with [specific thing they named]. The fix on their side is [honest workaround]. If it keeps happening, that is the problem we solve differently; no pitch, just saying it exists."
- Row 5 (problem described, no tool named): "How many [units] a week are you dealing with? Under [threshold] a spreadsheet is fine. Above it, the usual answers are [category]; I can list what to look for if you want."

Notice what the replies do not do: no link in the first reply, no "DM me", no claim about results you cannot prove. If the poster replies, continue in public for one more turn, then offer to move. Where to move is a real choice. If their bio links a LinkedIn profile, go there: a connection request that references the X post gets read, and the [LinkedIn message templates for buying signals](/blog/linkedin-message-templates-for-buying-signals) have a sequence for each signal type. If they have open DMs and have engaged with you, a short X DM that names the post is fine. Email is the last resort and only if the address is public.

Keep the volume human. X's limits page puts the technical ceiling at [500 direct messages sent per day and 400 follows per day](https://help.x.com/en/rules-and-policies/x-limits), with 50 original posts and 200 replies per day for unverified accounts. Those are the points at which X blocks you, not targets. Ten careful public replies a day is plenty, and an unverified account that posts nothing but replies to strangers looks like what it is.

## What does the 15-minute daily routine look like?

- Minutes 1 to 8: read your three private List timelines, newest first, then paste the five daily strings (rows 1 to 5) one by one, with `since:` set to yesterday, sorted by Latest. Open every post that is a real person asking or complaining. Expect three to ten candidates.
- Minutes 8 to 12: run the four-question triage on each candidate. Expect one to three to pass.
- Minutes 12 to 15: write one public reply per passing post, add the row to your sheet, and check yesterday's replies for responses.
- Once a week: run rows 6 to 8, review the sheet, and count conversations started and meetings booked from X alone.

After four weeks you have the only number that matters: how many conversations X produced per hour spent. If it is zero, stop; your buyers are not here. If it is one or more a week, keep going and consider the paid monitoring tools, now that you know which strings work.

## Where do these signals show up on LinkedIn?

The same eight behaviours happen on LinkedIn, often by the same people, with one difference: on LinkedIn most of the signal is engagement rather than text. Your buyer rarely posts "anyone recommend a [category] tool", but they do like and comment on a competitor's launch post, follow the competitor's page, or comment under an industry post about the pain. ## FAQ

### What counts as a buying signal on X?

A public post, reply or quote in which an identifiable person at a company asks for a recommendation, complains about a named vendor, compares named tools, announces a switch, describes a problem your product solves, or shows a change such as a hire or funding. The test is whether the account would be more likely than a random account to buy within a quarter; a like or a follow alone does not meet it.

### Can you find X buying signals for free?

Yes. X's search box with operators such as exact phrases, OR, exclusion, `since:` and `min_faves:` finds the posts described in this article at no cost while you are logged in. What you cannot get for free is alerting: saved searches work only in the X app, not on x.com, and third-party alert tools pay X per post read, so their plans start at a price.

### Should you reply in public or send a DM first?

Reply in public first. It is visible to everyone in the thread, it works even when the poster's DMs are closed, and it keeps you clear of X's rule against unsolicited bulk or automated DMs. Move to a DM, a LinkedIn message or email only after the poster has replied to you, and name the post when you do.

### How fast do you need to respond to a signal on X?

The same day. Recommendation requests collect answers within hours and the poster usually picks from the first few useful ones. Complaints and comparisons stay open longer, often a week or two, because the pain persists. Set `since:` to yesterday in your daily searches so you are always working the newest posts first.

### Can you automate X monitoring and outreach?

Monitoring, yes, with a paid tool or the X API; the searches in this article are what those tools run for you. Outreach, no. X's automation rules say you may not send unsolicited DMs in a bulk or automated manner, may not send automated replies based on keyword searches alone, and may not like posts automatically. Keep the reply human, and automate only the finding.

### X or LinkedIn for B2B buying signals?

Both, for different signals. X is better for text: candid complaints, recommendation requests and comparisons from founders, developers and marketers. LinkedIn is better for engagement: who liked or commented on a competitor's post, who follows their page, who reacted to a post about the pain, across almost every B2B industry. Run X by hand with the routine above and let a tool watch LinkedIn.
